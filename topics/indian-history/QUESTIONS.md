### "What ruined the Indus centres?" You said: the Ghaggar dried up; a tectonic shift re-routed the Yamuna to run parallel to the Ganga.
**Right, and more exact than my lesson page.** The book says the Yamuna "is supposed to have flowed through the Ghaggar valley
until an upheaval in the foothills of the Himalayas made it change its course"; it "now parallels the Ganga" [1]. My page said
the shift "sent its water into the Yamuna", which garbles that. Fixed in lesson 0002.
- **The piece you left out:** a *second* possible blow. Tectonic upheaval at the Indus mouth "may have produced a large lake
  submerging Mohenjo-Daro". Some scholars contest this, but the authors note even one blockage "would have done enough damage" [1].
- **The book's sentence to reuse:** "The drying up of the Ghaggar and the blocking of the lower Indus could thus have ruined the
  major centres of the Indus civilisation" [1]. Notice the "could" and the "may". These are hypotheses, not settled facts.

### "Why reject Hindu / Islamic / British?" Your answer
**Right on every point the authors make** [1]: the "Hindu period" wasn't homogeneous, Hindu traditions didn't disappear, Hindu–Muslim
cooperation mattered, and British rule was "an episode, though a very important one". You also added the political point (calling
Islamic and British rule two successive *foreign* periods has ugly present-day uses). The authors say this too: Hindu historians
"considered Islamic and British rule as two successive periods of foreign rule" [1]. That's your mission's "judge public claims"
showing up in lesson one.
- **One thing to sharpen:** they don't just relabel the periods as ancient, medieval and modern. They define each by its **political
  structure**: the *chakravartin* empires (ancient), competing regional "concentric states" (medieval), and the centralised Mughal
  state (modern) [1]. The label alone is no better than the old one; the structural definition is the argument.

### "Elephants change politics?" (left blank)
The book's answer [1]: chariots "did not lend themselves to monopolisation by a centralised power", but elephants did, because
"only a mighty ruler could afford to maintain adequate contingents of war elephants". From about 500 BC this favoured big states
like Magadha.

### "Were war elephants really that powerful?" (your surprise)
**Yes, with a big catch.**
- **Powerful:** at the Hydaspes (326 BC), Porus fielded roughly 85–100 elephants against Alexander. They "caused many losses"
  with iron-spiked tusks and trampling [2]. Chandragupta Maurya's gift of 500 elephants to Seleucus is, in Kulke & Rothermund's
  words, "one of the most important military aid transactions of the ancient world" [1]. At Ipsus (301 BC) those elephants blocked
  the enemy cavalry's return and decided the battle [2].
- **The catch:** wounded elephants, or ones whose driver was killed, tended to panic and run amok, "making casualties on either
  side". At the Hydaspes, "panicked and wounded elephants turned on the Indians themselves". Alexander's infantry opened their
  ranks, let the elephants through, and hit them with javelins [2].
- **Why kings kept paying anyway:** elephants were too costly to breed from birth, so Indian kings captured adults from the wild
  and trained them. Kings therefore protected elephant forests, and the war elephant lasted about 3,000 years and spread from
  India west to the Mediterranean and east to Southeast Asia. This is historian Thomas Trautmann's argument [3].

### "The chaturanga elephant moves in straight lines?"
**Probably not, but nobody knows for certain.** The rules of chaturanga are uncertain, and the elephant (*gaja*) most of all [4]:
- the usual reconstruction follows its Persian successor shatranj: a **diagonal leap of two squares**, jumping the square in between;
- al-Biruni (c. 1030, in his book *India*) describes one step forward or one step diagonally;
- a third proposal is an orthogonal two-square leap.

The piece became the **bishop**: its shatranj name, *alfil*, means "elephant", and Spanish still calls the bishop *alfil* [5]. The straight-line elephant you're
thinking of is the rook-like elephant of *chaturaji*, a four-player dice variant [4].

- **chess-king:** the king's movements are restricted because "if he is killed his army is vanquished even if it is still in good

## 2026-10-07 · Lesson 0003 "Judge the headline 'New DNA study debunks Aryan invasion theory'" (graded 0.6, regraded 0.75 the same day)
**Your answer (short):** the DNA was pooled because no single source had enough; it showed little or no Aryan DNA. But the
cities peaked c. 2300 BCE and declined in the drought of 1800–1500 BCE, before the Aryans arrived (Mitanni treaty, 2nd

**What's right:** every date checks out in K&R (mature stage "2300 to 2000 BC"; rainfall "by about 1800 to 1500 BC … well below
that of 3000 BC"; Mitanni treaty "about 1380 BC") [1]. Using dates to show the cities fell before the Aryans came is exactly the
argument that killed the invasion story. That's the specific evidence record 0002 asked for.

**What to fix:**
- **The pooling (regraded: not a fault).** The article you were given quotes David Reich: "each of the individual data sets did not
  produce enough DNA, pooling them resulted in sufficient genetic data" [4]. Reading that as pooling different sources is fair. The
  paper shows the data sets were 100+ sequencing attempts on **one** woman's skeleton [2], but the paper wasn't assigned, so
  my first grading held you to a source you weren't given (TEACHING-LOG 24). Your caution about a thin sample was right: n = 1.
- **What the result means (this one was on the lesson page).** "Little if any steppe ancestry" in a Harappan, when it's common in South Asia today, means that
  ancestry arrived **later**. The paper says Indo-European languages most naturally came "via Central Asia in the first half of the
  2nd millennium BCE" [2]. So the study *supports* a later migration; it only "debunks" an invasion theory that was already dead.
- **Your tone instinct was right, and well aimed.** The triumphant claims in the article are Vasant Shinde's ("completely reject",
  "very flimsy ground") [4], and his own paper concluded otherwise [2][3].
