::: free read-mehrgarh
What did the newer excavations (Mehrgarh in Baluchistan especially) change about the story of where this civilisation came from?
--- rubric
A long indigenous evolution: farming (barley, wheat) and herding at Mehrgarh from the 7th millennium BC, continuous for five millennia, moving from the Baluchistan hills into the Indus plains (Amri, 4th millennium). Contact with Mesopotamia was real, but "merely an extension of Mesopotamian civilisation" had to be rejected.

::: free read-bigcities
According to the authors, why were the **big** cities hit earlier and harder than small towns like Amri and Lothal when the climate changed?
--- rubric
The cities had no large army or administration to extract surplus from far away; their size matched the farm surplus of their immediate hinterland. When climate change cut that surplus they couldn't draw food from farther afield, so people left, and the shrinking population accelerated decline, hitting the biggest cities first.

### The story (from the reading)

In the 1920s archaeologists dug up **Harappa** and **Mohenjo-Daro**, two cities about 350 miles apart that looked as if one planner had drawn both: a raised, fortified **acropolis** to the west (Mohenjo-Daro's has the "Great Bath"), a gridded **lower city** with 30-foot main streets, a sewer connected to every house, and **standard bricks** whose length is twice the width and the width twice the height (1 : 2 : 4). They left thousands of carved stone **seals** with a **script nobody has deciphered**. Indus seals turn up in Mesopotamia, whose texts mention a trading partner called **Meluhha**, probably the Indus valley.

Suddenly India had a civilisation as old as Egypt's and Mesopotamia's, older than the Aryans, who until then had been treated as the creators of India's first culture.

### Why did the cities fall? (from the reading)

You met one cause in lesson 2: the **Yamuna changed course** and stopped feeding the **Ghaggar**, so cities like Kalibangan lost their farms. The chapter adds more, and they work together:

- **Nature:** a tectonic "dam" may have flooded the lower Indus (thick silt layers sit in Mohenjo-Daro's upper strata; standing water also brings malaria). Rainfall peaked around 2500 BC and then fell steeply.
- **People on the land:** overgrazing, and forests cut to feed countless kilns and fireplaces.
- **Economy and state:** the cities had no large army or bureaucracy to take food from far away. A city could only be as big as its own hinterland could feed. When harvests fell, people left, and the biggest cities emptied first.

::: categorize causes
Sort each cause of the decline into the authors' three kinds.
- The Yamuna shifts and the Ghaggar dries up > Nature
- Forests are cut for brick kilns and fireplaces > People on the land
- Cities cannot draw food from beyond their hinterland > Economy and state
--- explain
The book's point is that they compound: a natural shock hits a system that can only feed itself locally.
:::

### Judge a public claim: "The Aryans destroyed the Indus cities" (beyond the reading, with sources)

This is your mission's second skill: read a claim, then say what the evidence supports, what's disputed, and what's simply wrong.

**Where the claim came from.** In 1947 the archaeologist **Mortimer Wheeler** reported skeletons lying in Mohenjo-Daro's top layers and wrote: *"On circumstantial evidence, Indra stands accused"* [2]. Indra is the Rigveda's warrior god, praised as a "breaker of forts" [1]. Look at what the sentence does: it joins **ruins** (skeletons) to **texts** (hymns) without any date linking them. Textbooks repeated it for decades.

**What broke it.**
1. In 1964 **George Dales** argued the skeletons weren't one massacre: they came from different levels, so decades or centuries apart, and some wounds had healed [2][3].
2. K&R: no "last massacre" traces in any other centre; signs of slow internal decay everywhere; and the Vedic Aryans "most probably arrived in the Indus valley only centuries after its great cities had been extinguished" [1].

::: highlight hl-evidence
Which of these sentences (from or closely following K&R's chapter) are evidence **against** the claim that Aryan invaders destroyed the Indus cities?
--- passage
[[No traces of "last massacres" were found in any of the centres, apart from Mohenjo-Daro.]] The warrior god Indra was praised as a breaker of forts in many Vedic hymns. [[The Vedic Aryans most probably arrived in the Indus valley only centuries after its great cities had been extinguished.]] Scattered skeletons showing traces of violent death were found in the uppermost strata of Mohenjo-Daro. [[The excavations have revealed many striking symptoms of endogenous decay in those cities during the Late Harappan period.]]
--- explain
The marked three are evidence against. The Indra hymns and the skeletons are the *old* case **for** the claim: the first is a text with no date tying it to these ruins; the second is one site, and Dales later dated the bodies to different times.
:::

**The claim's live descendants.** The "Aryan question" is now a heated political debate in India [1]. Two opposite claims circulate:

- **"Aryans invaded and wiped out a native civilisation."** Rejected, as above.
- **"There were no Aryan migrants: the Indus civilisation was itself Aryan and Indo-European languages began in India."** K&R record this as one side of the debate, against the view that the Aryans were immigrants, probably in several waves [1]. Two pieces of evidence to weigh: horses, central to the Vedic world, are **never shown on Indus seals** and appear only in Late Harappan layers [1]; and the first genome from an Indus city (Rakhigarhi, 2019) had **"little if any Steppe pastoralist-derived ancestry"** [4], ancestry that is widespread in South Asia today. The authors conclude that Indo-European languages most naturally reached South Asia via Central Asia "in the first half of the 2nd millennium BCE" [4].

::: callout
**Say it this way:** "Speakers of Indo-Aryan languages most likely *migrated* into the northwest in the 2nd millennium BC, probably in waves; the Vedic Aryans came after the great Indus cities had already declined, mostly from environmental and economic causes."
**Not this:** "The Aryans invaded and destroyed the Indus Valley Civilisation." **Nor this:** "DNA proved there was no Aryan migration."
:::

### Optional: the rest of the Introduction (skim if you have time)

The Introduction's last sections, "The Regional Pattern of Indian History", "The Maritime Periphery and the Intrusion of European Powers" and "The Regional Pattern of Population Density", argue [1]:

- India has **three major regions** (the northern river plains, the southern highlands, the east coast) separated by a hard-to-cross **intermediate zone** (Gujarat to Orissa). That's why northern and southern great kings often coexisted without ever fighting.
- Most capitals vanished with their dynasties. **Delhi** (the gateway to the Ganga–Yamuna Doab) and **Patna/Pataliputra** were rebuilt again and again because of their strategic position.
- Indian rulers, focused on cavalry, ignored the sea. The British came in from **coastal bridgeheads** (Bombay, Calcutta, Madras) and "turned the pattern inside out": the periphery became the centre.
