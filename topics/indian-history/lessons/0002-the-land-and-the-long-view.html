According to the authors, what may have ruined the great centres of the Indus civilisation?

Environmental/tectonic change: the Ghaggar dried up (around 1700 BC, perhaps because a tectonic tilt made the Yamuna, which had flowed through the Ghaggar valley, change course), and the lower Indus may have been blocked, flooding Mohenjo-daro (contested). Outposts then decayed without the heartland's trade.

Why do the authors reject dividing Indian history into "Hindu, Islamic and British" periods, and what do they use instead?

It breeds misconceptions: the "Hindu period" wasn't uniform, Hindu traditions didn't disappear under Islamic or British rule, Hindu–Muslim cooperation mattered, British rule was an episode. Instead: ancient/medieval/modern by predominant political structure (chakravartin empires; regional concentric states; the centralised Mughal state onwards).

How did war elephants change Indian politics, in the authors' account?

Chariots couldn't be monopolised; elephants could, because only a mighty ruler could afford to maintain them. From about 500 BC they favoured large centralised powers like Magadha.

Block 2: the lesson

1 · Nature sets limits

The book opens with its thesis: "Nature sets limits, man transgresses them with his tools and his vision."1 Land that looks useless at one stage of technology becomes the best land at the next. Stone-tool hunters avoided the heavy soils that later peasants, with stronger ploughs and draught animals, turned into rich farmland. And wherever irrigated rice grew, "plenty of people could live and great empires could rise", until a change of climate or an invasion cut off that base.

The earliest example is the Indus civilisation. Rising rainfall from about 3000 BC let farmers cultivate the river valleys, and great cities like Mohenjo-daro and Harappa became centres of water management and trade. Then the land moved: the Ghaggar river dried up around 1700 BC, perhaps because a small tectonic tilt in the Himalayan foothills made the Yamuna, which had flowed through the Ghaggar valley, change course (it now runs parallel to the Ganga). Upheaval at the Indus mouth may also have flooded Mohenjo-daro, though some scholars contest this. The heartland was ruined before the Aryan herders arrived from the north.

Put these in the order the authors give them, earliest first.

Rainfall rises from about 3000 BC; the Ghaggar dries around 1700 BC; the Aryans arrive after the Indus civilisation had already lost its vigour; elephants enter warfare around 500 BC.

2 · How should history be divided?

Many historians used to divide Indian history into Hindu, Islamic and British periods. Kulke and Rothermund reject this. The "Hindu period" was never one tradition; those traditions didn't vanish under later rulers; Hindus and Muslims cooperated in politics, society and culture; and British rule, seen over the long run, was "an episode, though a very important one".1

They divide by political structure instead:

  • Ancient: empires of the chakravartin, the would-be universal ruler, who silenced rivals rather than governing them closely. The last was the Gupta empire.
  • Medieval: many regional states, each with a core region and a contested periphery. Islamic rulers fitted into this pattern more than they changed it.
  • Modern: begins with the Mughal empire, a highly centralised state built on land revenue and a huge army. The British conquered its remnants and continued its administration.

Sort each statement.

The authors classify by how power was organised, not by the rulers' religion or origin. The "two successive periods of foreign rule" framing is how the authors describe the view of Hindu historians who used the religious scheme.

In the authors' scheme, the modern period begins with the Mughal empire. Place its beginning on the timeline.

1526: Babur's victory at Panipat. Under the religious scheme, 1526 would be the middle of the "Islamic period"; under the authors' scheme, it's the start of the modern one, because the Mughal state was centralised in a new way.

3 · Elephants, chariots, and chess

Methods of war shaped politics. Aryan chariots could be used by any chief, so power stayed fragmented. War elephants, used from about 500 BC, were different: maintaining them cost so much that they favoured one big power, and the elephant-rich east gave rise to Magadha, the base of the first great empire. The authors add a detail you'll like: "Indian military strategy is faithfully reflected in the game of chess."1 The king commands from an elephant's back, and if he falls, the battle is lost however strong his army is.

Why did war elephants favour large, centralised powers?

Maintenance mattered more than supply: "Only a mighty ruler could afford to maintain adequate contingents of war elephants."

In both chess and the Indian battle the authors describe, why are the king's movements restricted?

"If he is killed his army is vanquished even if it is still in good condition." Same logic as checkmate.
