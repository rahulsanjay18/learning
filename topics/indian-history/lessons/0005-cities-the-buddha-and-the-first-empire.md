## Why this lesson
By about 1000 BC the Vedic people were farming the Ganga–Yamuna Doab and had no cities. Seven hundred years later a single state ruled
from Afghanistan to Bengal, with a capital that may have been the largest city in the world. Between those two points sit three
things this lesson connects: **new cities on the Ganges, a new religion (the Buddha's), and India's first empire (the Mauryas).**
The question to carry through: **why did all three happen in the same place, the eastern Ganges plain, at about the same time?**

<details><summary>Recap: the two earlier ages this lesson builds on</summary>

- **The Indus cities** (c. 2600–1700 BC): planned brick cities with standard weights, gone well before the Vedic hymns. After them, India had no
  cities for about a thousand years.
- **The Vedic age** (Rigveda c. 1300–1000 BC): our evidence is the priests' own hymns. By the Late Vedic age, the priests (Brahmins)
  and warrior-rulers (Kshatriyas) sat at the top of a ranked order of four **varnas** (estates); the many birth-castes, **jatis**,
  came later.
</details>

## Block 2: the lesson

### 1. Cities and the Buddha, together (from the reading)
Start from what's solid. After 600 BC the Ganges plain had cities again: five of the six big ones were capitals of
**mahajanapadas** ("great tribal realms", sixteen rival kingdoms and republics). Historians call this the **second urbanisation**
(the Indus cities were the first); K&R don't use the phrase, but it's the standard name you'll meet elsewhere.

Now the question: why would a new religion appear in exactly these places? Try to answer it before opening the steps. The first and third steps are from the reading; the second is beyond it.

::: worked
**Who was the Buddha talking back to?** K&R call Buddhism "a reaction to the Brahmin–Kshatriya alliance of the Late Vedic age". The
Vedic order put priests and their sacrifices at the top. A teaching that offered release from suffering through conduct, not ritual,
undercut the priests' monopoly.
--- step
**Who listened? (beyond the reading)** Coins and standard weights tell us there was money and long-distance trade, so the cities held
traders and craftsmen, people with wealth but no high place in the Vedic ranking. K&R don't spell this out, but they give the
clues: the Buddha preached in these towns and met kings like **Bimbisara of Magadha**, the towns soon held monasteries and stupas,
and Jainism, its sister movement, kept its base "in the rich merchant communities of western India".
--- step
**The dating clue runs the other way, too.** The earliest Buddhist stories already describe busy cities. Archaeology shows the Gangetic
cities only beginning in the 6th century BC and flourishing in the 5th, so that's one reason to move the Buddha later (the German
scholar Heinz Bechert's argument). The religion and
the cities are so tied together that each helps date the other.
--- step
**So:** K&R's own sentence is that the new Gangetic civilisation "found its spiritual expression" in a reform movement against the
Brahmin–Kshatriya alliance. The new urban society was the setting, and (beyond the reading) very likely the audience, for a teaching about
conduct rather than sacrifice.
:::

### 2. From kingdoms to an empire (from the reading, and the optional sections)
Magadha, one of the sixteen mahajanapadas, rose to dominate the Ganges plain within two generations, and its later rulers kept expanding. K&R list the steps: Bimbisara conquered
neighbouring Anga (and its access to iron ore and trade routes); his son Ajatashatru's generals fortified Pataligrama, a village on the Ganga that became
**Pataliputra** (today's Patna), about 380 km downriver (east) of Kausambi; the **Nandas** built the first huge army (Greek reports say 3,000 elephants); then **Chandragupta
Maurya** overthrew the last Nanda.

Alexander matters to this story mostly by what he *didn't* do. He crossed the Indus in 326 BC, beat Poros at the Hydaspes, and then his
army refused to go further east, where "more kings and war elephants were waiting". Indian sources don't even mention him. But Chandragupta, says tradition,
began by fighting the outposts Alexander left behind.

::: worked
**When did the Mauryas begin?** Work it out from three dates in the reading before looking.
--- step
Alexander's army was in the Panjab in 326 BC and the Nandas still ruled Magadha. By 317 BC the last Greek outposts in India were gone.
--- step
K&R: Chandragupta "seems to have usurped the throne of Magadha in **320 BC**" (many books say 321 or 322; the sources disagree by a year or two).
--- step
In **305 BC** Seleukos, heir to Alexander's eastern conquests, invaded; Chandragupta stopped him, and Seleukos ceded everything east of Kabul
for 500 elephants. The Maurya frontier was then roughly the Mughal frontier two thousand years later.
:::

### 3. Megasthenes: checking a foreign witness (from the reading)
Seleukos sent an ambassador, **Megasthenes**, to Pataliputra. His book is lost, but later Greek writers quoted it. He said the city's
palisade ran about 21 miles around, with 570 towers and 64 gates: twice the size of Marcus Aurelius's Rome. An ambassador has a motive to
exaggerate the court he was sent to. So how could a historian test him?

::: choice corrob-v1
Why do K&R take Megasthenes' size for Pataliputra more seriously than you might expect?
- [x] The spacing of towers he describes matches the spacing that an Indian text on statecraft prescribes
- [ ] The spacing of towers he describes matches the spacing that other Greek visitors reported much later
- [ ] The total wall length he describes matches the length that modern excavation has now fully traced
- [ ] The total wall length he describes matches the length that the inscriptions of Ashoka later record
--- hint
What kind of evidence, other than his own words, could check a number like this?
--- explain
D. Schlingloff showed that the gaps between towers and gates in Megasthenes' account match the 54 yards that the Arthashastra
prescribes. Two sources with different authors and purposes agree: that's **corroboration**, and it's why a historian's trust goes up.
:::

### 4. The Arthashastra's circle of kings (from the reading)
The **Arthashastra**, "the science of prosperity" (artha), is a manual for a king, traditionally by Chandragupta's minister
Kautilya. Its picture of the world is the **rajamandala**, the circle of kings. Reason it out first: if you're a king among many small
kingdoms, who is your natural enemy, and who is your natural friend?

::: worked
**Your neighbour is your enemy (ari).** You share a border, so you compete for the same land; either of you gains by swallowing the other.
--- step
**Your neighbour's neighbour is your friend (mitra).** The kingdom on the far side of your enemy has the same enemy you do. "The enemy of my
enemy". Kautilya: "one with territory separated by one [other territory] is … the ally."
--- step
**The pattern repeats outward:** beyond the ally lies the enemy's ally, then the ally's ally, then the enemy's ally's ally. In front and
behind you, enemies and friends alternate.
--- step
**Two kings break the pattern.** The **middle king** (madhyama) borders both you and your enemy and is strong enough to stay neutral or decide
your war by joining one side. The **neutral king** (udasina, "the great outsider") sits outside the whole circle, stronger than any of you,
and can interfere anywhere: K&R say he "was to be carefully watched".
:::

::: diagram kind=graph dir=right caption="The rajamandala along one line, as Kautilya lists it: borders alternate enemy and friend (Arthashastra VI.2, quoted in K&R)."
- you: You, the would-be conqueror
- ari: Enemy (ari)
- mitra: Ally (mitra)
- arimitra: Enemy's ally
- mm: Ally's ally
- you > ari: borders
- ari > mitra: borders
- mitra > arimitra: borders
- arimitra > mm: borders
:::

This is the same "enemy of my enemy" logic you'll meet in the strategy course as balance-of-power thinking.

The king wins by building up his seven factors of power (himself, ministers, provinces, city, treasury, army, allies) and wearing down
the enemy's *before* any battle: irrigation, trade, mines, forts, elephants, and spies.

::: number mandala-v1 answer=4
Kingdoms lie in a row. You are at one end. Counting outward, the 1st kingdom is your neighbour. By Kautilya's pattern, which is the
**nearest kingdom beyond your ally that is also friendly to you**? Type its position in the row (1 = your neighbour).
--- explain
1 enemy, 2 ally (mitra), 3 the enemy's ally, 4 the ally's ally. Friends sit at even positions, enemies at odd ones.
:::

::: exact mandala-v2 answer="madhyama|middle king|the middle king|madhyama king"
Kingdoms A and B, neighbours, are at war. Kingdom C borders both and is strong enough to stay out of it or to decide it by joining
either side. What does Kautilya call C? (Either the Sanskrit term or its English translation.)
--- explain
The **middle king** (madhyama). The neutral king (udasina) is different: he sits outside the circle altogether.
:::

### 5. The claim: "Chanakya was India's Machiavelli"
You'll hear this often. The sociologist Max Weber made the strongest version in 1919: next to the Arthashastra, he said, Machiavelli's
*The Prince* is harmless. Judge it from the reading. Keep in mind three facts K&R give: spies take up many pages; the text's real
focus is a prosperous economy run by the state; and the king must keep his subjects content, because high taxes and forced labour
"would drive the population into the arms of the enemy".

Beyond the reading, one more caution about the name: the Arthashastra calls its author Kautilya, and tradition makes him the same man
as Chanakya, Chandragupta's minister. Many scholars now doubt that. K&R keep the older view: Kautilya lived around 300 BC and later hands added to his text, some as
late as AD 300. The newer view (Patrick Olivelle's 2013 edition) puts the composition of most of the text itself in the first centuries AD.

::: free mach-claim-v1
Using the reading, give (1) **one way the comparison with Machiavelli fits**, (2) **one way K&R say it misleads**, and (3) **one reason
the Arthashastra may not describe Chandragupta's real empire.** Then give your verdict on the claim, in a sentence.
--- rubric
Grades exactly the three named parts, from K&R Ch. 2 only.
(1) Fits: detailed, amoral advice on spies and secret agents with "great psychological insight into the weakness of human nature";
"no moral limits" to exploiting the surplus; the enemy/ally logic is pure power calculation. Any one.
(2) Misleads: K&R say Kautilya "paid less attention to clandestine activities in the enemy's territory than to the elimination of
'thorns' in the king's own country"; most of the book is economics and administration (mining, trade, crafts, agriculture, salaries);
and it limits power by feasibility (subjects' welfare and contentment). Any one.
(3) Not a picture of the empire: it describes many rival small kingdoms, like the mahajanapada period before Magadha's victory, so it's
normative and about state formation; parts were added later (to AD 300). Either.
Verdict: any reasoned verdict. Got it = all three parts, no false statement. (Authorship doubts are beyond the reading: credit, never required.)
:::
