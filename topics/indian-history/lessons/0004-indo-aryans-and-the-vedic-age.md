## Why this lesson
The Indus cities were gone by about 1700 BC. The next people we can name, the Vedic Aryans, left no cities and no inscriptions in
India, only hymns their priests memorised. So everything in this lesson rests on one question: **what can a book of priestly hymns
tell a historian, and what can't it?** Caste is the test case, because the oldest description of the four varnas is one of those hymns.

## Warm-up: how the Indus civilisation began (5-minute re-teach)
Last lesson, the order of the Indus stages didn't land. Here's why the order matters. The old story said the cities were outposts
of Mesopotamia, suddenly planted. **The stages are the evidence against that:** if the cities grew out of local villages over
thousands of years, they weren't planted from outside [1].

::: timeline range=-7000:-1500 events="c-7000:farming at Mehrgarh, in the hills|c-4000:villages on the plain (Amri)|c-1800:squatters in the ruins" spans="-2600:-1700:the great cities" caption="K&R: Mehrgarh's farming goes back to the 7th millennium BC, settlements spread into the Indus valley in the 4th millennium, and the cities flourished 2600–1700 BC [1]."
:::

Read it as a story of moving downhill and growing: **hills → plain → cities → decline.** Farmers in the Baluchistan hills (Mehrgarh)
came down onto the Indus plain (Amri), the villages grew into planned cities, and at the end people camped in the ruins.

::: order stages-2
Put these in order, earliest first.
- Barley and wheat farming in the Baluchistan hills
- Villages settle down on the Indus plain
- Planned cities with standard-sized bricks
- People camp in the ruins, reusing broken bricks
--- explain
Hills → plain → cities → decline: Mehrgarh (from the 7th millennium BC), Amri (4th millennium), the Mature cities (2600–1700 BC),
then Late Harappan squatters. A slow local growth, which is why "an outpost of Mesopotamia" was dropped [1].
:::

## Block 2: the lesson

### What the Rigveda can tell us (from the reading)
Start from what's solid. K&R put it in two sentences: the Vedic texts, "and in particular the Rigveda, still remain our major source
concerning the early phases of Vedic culture", but "these texts express the priestly world-view of the Brahmins" [1]. Both are true
at once. Oral transmission kept the words very accurate, so we know what the priests sang. But we only hear the priests.

::: categorize rv-limits
Can the Rigveda alone answer this, or do we need another kind of source?
- Which rivers the early Vedic people knew > The Rigveda can tell us
- What the Dasas thought of the newcomers > Needs another source
- That cattle raids were a common way to fight > The Rigveda can tell us
- The year the first Aryans crossed into India > Needs another source
--- explain
The hymns name rivers (the Kubha and Suvastu, today's Kabul and Swat) and call warfare goshati, "getting cattle" [1]. They give only
the Aryan priests' side, so the Dasas' view is lost, and they carry no dates: dates have to come from outside the text.
:::

### Dating a book that has no dates
If the hymns carry no dates, how does anyone date them? Try it before you look: what kinds of outside evidence could pin down when a
text was composed?

::: worked
**Clue 1: a treaty outside India.** Around **1380 BC** a Mitanni king in upper Mesopotamia signed a treaty with the Hittite king
Suppiluliuma I that calls on the Vedic gods Mitra, Varuna, Indra and the Nasatyas [1]. So the gods, and the language of the hymns,
already existed by then.
--- step
**Clue 2: a metal.** The later hymns mention iron, and iron came into use in South Asia around 1000 BC. So the latest parts of the
Rigveda "cannot be much older than the eleventh century BC" [1].
--- step
**Clue 3: counting back from the Buddha.** Max Müller started from the Buddha (c. 500 BC). The Upanishads come before Buddhist
philosophy, the Brahmanas before them, and the hymns before those. That gives about 1200–1000 BC for the hymns. Scholars still mostly
accept his chain, with the Rigveda stretched to **1300–1000 BC** [1].
--- step
**Put together:** the hymns were composed roughly between the 14th and 11th centuries BC. K&R's chronology calls c. 1400–900 BC the
Early Vedic period, and after about 1000 BC, with iron and settled farming in the Ganga–Yamuna Doab, the Late Vedic age [1].
:::

::: diagram kind=graph caption="How the Rigveda is dated: three outside clues, one conclusion (K&R Ch. 1)."
- mit: 1380 BC: Mitanni treaty names Indra, Varuna, Mitra
- iron: Late hymns mention iron, in use c. 1000 BC
- bud: The Buddha, c. 500 BC
- rv: Rigveda composed c. 1300–1000 BC
- mit > rv: gods and language already exist
- iron > rv: latest parts ≥ 11th c. BC
- bud > rv: count back through Upanishads, Brahmanas
:::

--- explain
c. 1380 BC: about three centuries after the Indus cities ended, and the oldest dated evidence of the Vedic gods anywhere [1].

### Varna and jati
Two words get translated as "caste", and the claim at the end depends on keeping them apart. Both definitions are K&R's [1]:
- **Varna** first meant *colour*, the badge separating the free Aryans from the subjugated people. It became the name of four broad
  **estates**: priests (Brahmin), warriors (Kshatriya), free peasants and traders (Vaishya), and labourers and artisans (Shudra).
- **Jati** is the caste one is **born** into (*jata*, "born"): the many individual castes. The varnas later served as umbrella
  categories for them.

The four varnas first appear in one hymn, the **Purusha hymn**, Rigveda X, 90 [1]:

> The Brahman was his mouth, of both arms was the Rajanya [Kshatriya] made.
> His thighs became the Vaishya, from his feet the Shudra was produced.

::: choice purusha-date
What does the hymn's location, book X, tell you about when the fourfold varna order appears in the sources?
- [ ] It appears in the oldest hymns, right from the start of Vedic settlement
- [x] It appears in the latest hymns, after a long stretch of Vedic settlement
- [ ] It appears in the middle hymns, during the first battles with the Dasas
- [ ] It appears in the latest hymns, after the Vedic people had reached Mesopotamia
--- explain
Books II–VII are the oldest; book X is late and "contains … evidence of the caste system which is missing in the earlier books" [1].
The early hymns distinguish only ordinary tribesmen (vish), warrior nobles (kshatriya) and priests.
:::

### The claim
You'll hear: **"Caste is unchanged since Vedic times."** Judge it from the reading only.

::: free caste-claim
Using only the reading, say (1) **what about caste was already present** in the Vedic texts, (2) **what came only later**, and
(3) **one reason the Purusha hymn may describe an ideal rather than how society actually worked.** Then give your verdict on the claim.
--- rubric
Grades exactly the three named parts (TEACHING-LOG 25), from K&R Ch. 1 only.
(1) Present in Vedic times: a ranked order of estates (Brahmin and Kshatriya on top, then Vaishya, then Shudra), stated in the late
Purusha hymn; artisans despised as Shudras, and ritual impurity already strong (vessels for the Agnihotra made only by Aryans). Any one.
(2) Later: the full jati system, "this full-fledged caste system assumed greater importance only at a much later period"; also the
fourfold order itself is absent from the earliest hymns (books II–VII). Either.
(3) Ideal vs. reality: the hymns give "the priestly world-view of the Brahmins", and the hymn legitimised the Brahmins' place at the
apex: the authors are the ones ranked first. Or: a single late hymn, mythical, not a description.
Verdict: false as stated (it changed: from three groups, to four estates, to many jatis), though some ideas (hierarchy, impurity)
are very old. Got it = all three parts with no false statement; a reasoned verdict either way is fine.
:::
