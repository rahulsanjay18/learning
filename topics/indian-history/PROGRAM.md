# Indian History: the major (draft program)

*Drafted 2026-10-05. A part-time "major" at 2 blocks a week (see `notes/study-plan.md`). Mission set 2026-10-05 (`MISSION.md`).*


## Levels and requirement groups (revised 2026-10-06; structure: `notes/major-design.md`, adapted from NYU Gallatin)
- **Level I (≈ BA):** IH100 the arc → IH150 how historians work → IH201–206 the eras (K&R) · breadth: IH310 religion and
  philosophy, IH320 war and the state, IH330 economic history, IH340 art and literature · practice: IH390 sites, museums and a
  family oral history · IH398 colloquium · IH400 research essay.
- **Level II (≈ graduate):** IH510 historiography seminar · IH520 primary sources in translation · IH530 regional histories ·
  electives IH540 Partition and memory, IH550 a source language (optional) · IH596 research practicum · IH598 colloquium · IH599 thesis.
- **Books to ask for:** an economic history of India (IH330); historiography readers for IH510 (to choose together).
The full map with prerequisites is `curriculum.json` (each course has a `level` and a `group`: core, breadth, elective,
practice, capstone, colloquium). Each level ends with a **colloquium**: a 20–25 work List of Works, a 1,500–2,500 word
rationale, and a 60-minute conversation, graded on the rubric in `notes/major-design.md`.

## TL;DR
- **Spine:** Kulke & Rothermund, *A History of India* (grade A, in collection). Its 8 chapters become the core courses.
- **Each lesson** (one 25-minute block): read a short slice with a reading guide → a lesson page that tells the story, places it on a
  **timeline and map**, and puts one **primary source** in your hands → practice → spaced review.
- **Deeper than "read, lecture, quiz"** in three ways:
  1. **Thinking like a historian:** sourcing, contextualizing, corroborating and close reading of primary sources [1].
  2. **Historiography:** where historians disagree, and why.
  3. **Writing:** a short argued answer at the end of each unit, graded with a rubric.

## 2. One lesson (a 25-minute block)
1. **Warm-up (2 min):** 2–3 retrieval questions from earlier lessons.
2. **Reading (~10 min):** a 6–10 page slice of Kulke & Rothermund with a **reading guide**: three questions to answer while reading, and
   "what is the authors' argument here?" For longer readings, a whole block is reading and the next is the lesson page.
3. **Lesson page (~8 min):** the story in a few paragraphs, then:
   - **timeline** placement and **map** (where it happened);
   - **one primary source** excerpt (e.g. Ashoka's inscriptions or Babur's memoirs, both quoted in Kulke & Rothermund) with sourcing questions: *who wrote this, when, for whom, why, and can we trust it?*;
   - a **"why did this happen?"** question (causes, not just events).
4. **Practice (~5 min):** timeline-place, map-locate, ordering, cause/effect sorting, recall, one short free response that I grade.
5. **Afterwards:** misses go into the daily review deck automatically.

## 3. Every unit (~5 lessons)
- **Unit check:** recall-heavy (typed answers, timelines and maps, not multiple choice), so guesses can't inflate it.
- **A historian's answer:** ~300 words on a big question (e.g. "Why did Ashoka turn to dhamma?", "Why did the Mughal Empire decline?").
  Rubric: a clear claim, evidence, context, and a counter-argument considered.
- **A debate:** two historians' views side by side; you decide which is better supported and why.

## 4. Reference documents (grow with the course)
- **Master timeline** (every event from every lesson) and **map** of places.
- **Dynasty table:** who, when, where, capital, key ruler.
- **Glossary:** dharma, jati, varna, zamindar, mansabdar, dhamma, swaraj… Once it exists, every lesson uses its terms.

## 5. Wisdom (communities)
Historians discuss and argue; to be chosen with you. Candidates: moderated history Q&A forums, a local or online reading group,
museums. Skipped if you'd rather not.

## 6. Mission (answered 2026-10-05; see MISSION.md)
Why: heritage and family, understanding India today, curiosity. Success: tell the whole arc with causes; judge public claims
about history. Interested in every era and in cross-era themes. Consequences for the program:
- **Historiography moves up.** Each unit's "debate" (§3) pairs historians' views with **a present-day public claim** to judge
  (e.g. Aryan migration in the Introduction; Mughal rule in IH 203; Partition in IH 205). This is the practice for "judge history debates".
- **Causes over names.** Every lesson keeps its "why did this happen?" question; unit checks ask for causes in recall form.

Original questions (kept for reference):
1. **Why Indian History?** Heritage and family, understanding today's India (politics, religion), travel, or plain curiosity?
2. **What would "success" look like** in a year? E.g. explain the arc to a friend; read a newspaper debate about history and judge it; visit sites and know what you're seeing.
3. **Any era or theme you're most drawn to** (ancient, Mughals, colonial, independence; religion, war, art, economy)?
4. **How do you read the book**: e-reader, laptop, paper? (It changes how readings are assigned.)
