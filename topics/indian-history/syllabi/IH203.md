## Course description
The Mughal Empire, from Babur's victory at Panipat in 1526 to the empire's breakup in the eighteenth century, together with its
rivals and successors: the Rajputs, the Sikhs, the Marathas, and the European trading companies. The central question is why an
empire this rich and powerful fragmented so fast after 1707. Historians have answered in four main ways: religion, an agrarian
and fiscal crisis, regional power shifting, and Europe. You'll weigh all four.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this course you can:
1. Explain why Babur won at Panipat and how Akbar turned a conquest into a lasting state.
2. Explain the mansabdari system and the land-revenue system and how they connected army, nobility and peasants.
3. Describe the Mughals' relations with the Rajputs, Sikhs and Marathas.
4. Explain the European trading companies' rise and the shift from land power to sea power.
5. Compare the main explanations of Mughal decline and defend one, using the "decline or decentralization?" debate.
6. Read a Mughal source (the *Baburnama*, the *Ain-i-Akbari*) critically.

## Texts
- **Required:** Kulke and Rothermund, Chapter 5 "The Rise and Fall of the Mughal Empire". In your library.
- **Required (to buy):** John F. Richards, *The Mughal Empire* (New Cambridge History of India, 1993), by topic: Akbar's state, the mansabdari system, Aurangzeb's reign, and the empire's end. Eaton, *India in the Persianate Age*, the Mughal chapters (the book's Chapters 5–7).
- **Recommended:** Bose and Jalal, *Modern South Asia*, 4th ed. (to buy), Chapter 4 "The Mughal Empire: state, economy and society" and Chapter 5 "India between empires: decline or decentralization?".
- **In your library:** Grewal, *The Sikhs of the Punjab*; Pearson, *The Portuguese in India*; Cleveland Beach, *Mughal and Rajput Painting* (all New Cambridge History of India).
- **Primary sources:** the *Baburnama* (Thackston's translation, 1996), on Panipat; the *Ain-i-Akbari* (Blochmann and Jarrett's translation, public domain), on the mansabs.
