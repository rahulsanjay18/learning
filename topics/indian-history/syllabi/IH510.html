Course description

A graduate seminar on how Indian history has been written. Each week covers one school, reading a founding text alongside a modern assessment: colonial and Orientalist scholarship (Mill, Jones, Müller), nationalist history, Marxist history (Kosambi, R. S. Sharma), the Cambridge school, Subaltern Studies, and recent debates. By the end you should be able to identify a historian's school from a page of their writing, and say what that school sees well and what it misses.

Learning objectives

By the end of this course you can:

  1. Describe each school's core assumptions, questions and methods.
  2. Explain how each school read the same events (1857, the national movement, Partition) differently.
  3. Evaluate Said's critique of Orientalism and its application to India.
  4. Identify a historian's school from their writing and assess their argument on its own terms.
  5. Write a historiographical essay that traces a debate across schools.

Texts

  • Required: James Mill, The History of British India (1817), the periodization and "Hindu civilization" chapters (public domain). Edward Said, Orientalism (1978), Introduction. Thomas Trautmann, Aryans and British India (1997), selected chapters. D. D. Kosambi, An Introduction to the Study of Indian History (1956), Chapter 1. R. S. Sharma, Indian Feudalism (1965), introduction. Bipan Chandra et al., India's Struggle for Independence (1988), introduction. Anil Seal, The Emergence of Indian Nationalism (1968), introduction. C. A. Bayly, Indian Society and the Making of the British Empire (New Cambridge History, 1988), introduction. Ranajit Guha, "On Some Aspects of the Historiography of Colonial India" (Subaltern Studies I, 1982). Dipesh Chakrabarty, Provincializing Europe (2000), Introduction. Romila Thapar, The Past Before Us (2013), introduction.
  • Recommended (in your library): Young, Postcolonialism: A Very Short Introduction.
