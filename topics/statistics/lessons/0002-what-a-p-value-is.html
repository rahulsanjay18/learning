What a p-value is (and isn't)

About 20 minutes. One win: read any p-value correctly, and know what it can't tell you.

The situation

You A/B test a new checkout page, 12,000 users per arm. Conversion goes from 5.0% to 5.6%. The test reports \(z \approx 2.08\), two-sided \(p \approx 0.038\). Your PM asks: "So there's a 96% chance the new page is better?"

No. And you already know why, from the pretest question you got right.

First, the vocabulary

A hypothesis test is a decision rule. Four terms make it work:

  • \(H_0\), the null hypothesis: the boring explanation. Here: the new page makes no difference.
  • \(H_1\), the alternative: there is a real effect.
  • \(\alpha\) (alpha), the significance level: a threshold you choose before looking at the data, usually 0.05. The rule: reject \(H_0\) if \(p \le \alpha\).1
  • Power: the probability that the test rejects \(H_0\) when there really is an effect of a given size.1

Since the truth is either \(H_0\) or \(H_1\), and you either reject or don't, there are four outcomes and two ways to be wrong. Casella & Berger note that these "traditionally have been given the non-mnemonic names" Type I and Type II.5

Type I = rejecting a true \(H_0\) (false alarm, rate \(\alpha\)). Type II = missing a real effect (rate \(\beta\)). Power is the chance of "the page works and your test detects it".

The definition

The p-value is the probability, assuming the null hypothesis is true, of getting a result at least as extreme as the one observed.

$$p = P(\text{result this extreme or more} \mid H_0)$$

Bruce, Bruce & Gedeck put it as "the probability that, given a chance model, results as extreme as the observed results could occur" (p. 108).1

In the A/B test, \(H_0\) says "the new page makes no difference". Under \(H_0\) the test statistic \(z\) is roughly standard normal. The p-value is the area in the tails beyond the \(z\) you observed. Move the slider:

Notice what the area is made of: outcomes in a world where the new page does nothing. It describes the data under one assumption. It says nothing directly about whether that assumption is true.

Why it isn't \(P(H_0 \mid \text{data})\)

On the pretest you got the disease problem right: a test with a 5% false-positive rate, \(P(+ \mid \text{healthy}) = 0.05\), still left \(P(\text{disease} \mid +)\) at only about 15%, because the disease was rare. Flipping a conditional needs the base rate.

A p-value is the same kind of quantity as that 5%: \(P(\text{data} \mid H_0)\). Turning it into \(P(H_0 \mid \text{data})\) needs a base rate too: how often your ideas actually work. A small p-value from a long shot is like a positive test for a rare disease.3

Worked example (click through it): of every 100 product ideas you A/B test, 10 truly work. You test at \(\alpha = 0.05\), with power 0.8. Among the results that come out significant, what fraction are duds?

Split the 100 ideas by the truth: 90 duds (\(H_0\) true) and 10 that really work (\(H_1\) true).

Duds that come out significant are Type I errors, at rate \(\alpha\): \(90 \times 0.05 = 4.5\).

Real effects that come out significant are detected at rate = power: \(10 \times 0.8 = 8\).

Among all significant results, the fraction of duds is \(\frac{4.5}{4.5+8} = 0.36\). Over a third of your "wins" are noise, even though every one had \(p < 0.05\). It's the disease problem again: a rare "condition" (ideas that work) plus a false-positive rate.

Your turn, different numbers: 200 ideas, 20% truly work, \(\alpha = 0.05\), but your tests are underpowered at power 0.5. What fraction of significant results are duds? (A decimal.)

Duds: 160, and \(160 \times 0.05 = 8\) significant. Real: 40, and \(40 \times 0.5 = 20\) significant. Duds \(= \frac{8}{28} \approx 0.29\).

What a p-value can't tell you

The American Statistical Association's 2016 statement lists six principles.2 The three that matter most day to day:

  1. P-values do not measure the probability that the hypothesis is true, or that the data came from chance alone.
  2. A p-value does not measure the size of an effect or its importance. With huge samples, tiny effects get tiny p-values.
  3. Decisions shouldn't rest only on whether \(p\) crosses a threshold.

Why a small p isn't a big effect

Effect size is how big the difference is, in units you care about. In the A/B test: the lift is 5.6% − 5.0% = 0.6 percentage points (absolute), or 0.6/5.0 = a 12% relative increase. It answers "how much?", which a p-value never does. Lesson 3 covers it properly, along with how to judge whether a size matters.

The p-value comes from \(z = \dfrac{\text{observed effect}}{\text{standard error}}\), and the standard error shrinks like \(1/\sqrt{n}\). So \(z\) gets large, and \(p\) small, either because the effect is big or because there's a lot of data. The p-value can't tell you which.

Intuition: a coin that lands heads 50.5% of the time. In 100 flips its bias adds half a head against about ±5 heads of noise: invisible (\(p \approx 0.9\)). In a million flips it adds 5,000 heads against about ±500 of noise: unmistakable (\(z \approx 10\)). The bias grows like \(n\), the noise only like \(\sqrt n\), so with enough data any nonzero effect becomes "significant". Significant means detectably not zero, not big.

Study C is "highly significant" for a lift nobody should care about. Study A may have found something big but doesn't have enough data to be sure. So always report the effect size next to \(p\). Confidence intervals (lesson 3) show both at once.

Practice

Back to the A/B test: \(p = 0.038\). Which sentence is correct?

The p-value is computed in the world where \(H_0\) is true. The first and fourth options are \(P(H_0 \mid \text{data})\) in disguise; the third is its complement.

Sort each statement about what \(p\) means.

Only the first stays inside the "no-effect world". The other two flip the conditional into \(P(H_0 \mid \text{data})\), or its complement.

Sort each statement about \(p\) and effect size.

\(p\) mixes effect size with sample size (\(z = \text{effect}/\text{SE}\)), so a smaller \(p\) doesn't mean a bigger effect: see studies A–C above.

Find the \(z\) where the one-sided tail is 0.025, so a two-sided test at \(\alpha = 0.05\) just rejects. Move the slider, then press Check.

\(z = 1.96\): each tail holds 2.5%, together 5%. That's where "1.96" comes from.

From memory: in one plain sentence your PM would understand, what does \(p = 0.038\) mean here?

"If the new page truly made no difference, we'd see a lift this big or bigger only about 4% of the time, so the data are hard to square with 'no difference'. That alone doesn't tell us how likely the page is to be better, or by how much."
