What a p-value is (and isn't)

About 15 minutes. One win: read any p-value correctly, and know what it can't tell you.

The situation

You A/B test a new checkout page, 12,000 users per arm. Conversion goes from 5.0% to 5.6%. The test reports \(z \approx 2.08\), two-sided \(p \approx 0.038\). Your PM asks: "So there's a 96% chance the new page is better?"

No. And you already know why, from the pretest question you got right.

The definition

The p-value is the probability, assuming the null hypothesis is true, of getting a result at least as extreme as the one observed.

$$p = P(\text{result this extreme or more} \mid H_0)$$

Bruce, Bruce & Gedeck put it as "the probability that, given a chance model, results as extreme as the observed results could occur" (p. 108).1

In the A/B test, \(H_0\) says "the new page makes no difference". Under \(H_0\) the test statistic \(z\) is roughly standard normal. The p-value is the area in the tails beyond the \(z\) you observed. Move the slider:

Notice what the area is made of: outcomes in a world where the new page does nothing. It describes the data under one assumption. It says nothing directly about whether that assumption is true.

Why it isn't \(P(H_0 \mid \text{data})\)

On the pretest you got the disease problem right: a test with a 5% false-positive rate, \(P(+ \mid \text{healthy}) = 0.05\), still left \(P(\text{disease} \mid +)\) at only about 15%, because the disease was rare. Flipping a conditional needs the base rate.

A p-value is the same kind of quantity as that 5%: \(P(\text{data} \mid H_0)\). Turning it into \(P(H_0 \mid \text{data})\) needs a base rate too: how often your ideas actually work. A small p-value from a long shot is like a positive test for a rare disease.3

Of every 100 product ideas you A/B test, 10 truly work. You use \(\alpha = 0.05\), and your tests have power 0.8 (they detect a real effect 80% of the time). Among the results that come out significant, what fraction are duds? (A decimal.)

Duds: \(90 \times 0.05 = 4.5\) significant. Real: \(10 \times 0.8 = 8\) significant. Fraction of duds \(= \frac{4.5}{4.5+8} = 0.36\). Over a third of your "wins" are noise, even though every one had \(p < 0.05\).

What a p-value can't tell you

The American Statistical Association's 2016 statement lists six principles.2 The three that matter most day to day:

  1. P-values do not measure the probability that the hypothesis is true, or that the data came from chance alone.
  2. A p-value does not measure the size of an effect or its importance. With huge samples, tiny effects get tiny p-values.
  3. Decisions shouldn't rest only on whether \(p\) crosses a threshold.

Practice

Back to the A/B test: \(p = 0.038\). Which sentence is correct?

The p-value is computed in the world where \(H_0\) is true. The first and fourth options are \(P(H_0 \mid \text{data})\) in disguise; the third is its complement.

Sort each statement.

Everything correct here is about the data under \(H_0\). Every misreading tries to make \(p\) say something about \(H_0\) itself, or about effect size.

Find the \(z\) where the one-sided tail is 0.025, so a two-sided test at \(\alpha = 0.05\) just rejects. Move the slider, then press Check.

\(z = 1.96\): each tail holds 2.5%, together 5%. That's where "1.96" comes from.

From memory: in one plain sentence your PM would understand, what does \(p = 0.038\) mean here?

"If the new page truly made no difference, we'd see a lift this big or bigger only about 4% of the time, so the data are hard to square with 'no difference'. That alone doesn't tell us how likely the page is to be better, or by how much."
