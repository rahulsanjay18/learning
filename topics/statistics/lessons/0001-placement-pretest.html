1 · Probability

A disease affects 1% of people. A test catches 90% of cases and gives a false positive for 5% of healthy people. You test positive. What's the probability you have the disease? (A number; 2 decimals is fine.)

Bayes: \(P(D\mid +) = \frac{0.01 \cdot 0.9}{0.01 \cdot 0.9 + 0.99 \cdot 0.05} = \frac{0.009}{0.0585} \approx 0.154\). Most positives come from the large healthy group.

\(A\) and \(B\) are two independent events in the same experiment (same sample space), with \(P(A)=0.3\) and \(P(B)=0.5\). What is \(P(A \cup B)\), the probability that at least one of them happens?

Inclusion–exclusion, with \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)\) by independence: \(0.3+0.5-0.15 = 0.65\).

2 · Random variables

\(X\) and \(Y\) are independent with \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=4\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)=9\). What is \(\operatorname{Var}(2X - Y)\)?

\(\operatorname{Var}(aX+bY) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X) + b^2\operatorname{Var}(Y)\) for independent variables: \(4 \cdot 4 + 1 \cdot 9 = 25\). The minus sign doesn't reduce variance.

You repeat independent trials, each succeeding with probability \(p\), until the first success. What is the expected number of trials (in terms of \(p\))?

Geometric distribution: \(E[N] = \sum_{k\ge1} k(1-p)^{k-1}p = 1/p\). With \(p=0.25\) you expect 4 tries.

3 · Distributions

Server errors arrive as a Poisson process averaging 2 per hour. What's the probability of zero errors in a given hour? (Exact form or a decimal.)

\(P(X=0) = \frac{\lambda^0 e^{-\lambda}}{0!} = e^{-2} \approx 0.135\).

Roughly what share of a normal distribution lies within 2 standard deviations of the mean?

About 95% (more precisely 95.4%). The 68–95–99.7 rule covers 1, 2 and 3 SDs.

Which continuous distribution is memoryless: having waited \(t\) already doesn't change how much longer you expect to wait?

Exponential: \(P(T > s+t \mid T > s) = P(T > t)\). It's the continuous counterpart of the geometric distribution.

4 · Sampling and the CLT

You average 100 independent measurements, each with standard deviation 15. What is the standard deviation of the average?

Standard error \(= \sigma/\sqrt{n} = 15/10 = 1.5\).

For large \(n\), the Central Limit Theorem says which of these is approximately normal (for most well-behaved populations)?

The sampling distribution of the sample mean. The raw data keep whatever shape the population has.

5 · Estimation

Why divide by \(n-1\) instead of \(n\) in the sample variance \(s^2\)?

With \(n-1\), \(E[s^2] = \sigma^2\). Using \(\bar x\) instead of the true mean makes the squared deviations too small on average, and \(n-1\) corrects that. (Both versions are consistent.)

You see \(k\) successes in \(n\) independent Bernoulli(\(p\)) trials. What is the maximum likelihood estimate of \(p\)?

Maximize \(\ell(p) = k\ln p + (n-k)\ln(1-p)\): \(\frac{k}{p} - \frac{n-k}{1-p} = 0 \Rightarrow \hat p = k/n\).

A study reports a 95% confidence interval for a mean. Which reading is correct?

Confidence is a property of the procedure: across repeated samples, 95% of the intervals it produces cover the true mean. Any one interval either contains it or not. The "95% probability" reading needs a Bayesian credible interval.

6 · Hypothesis testing

What is a p-value?

\(P(\text{test statistic at least this extreme} \mid H_0)\). It is not \(P(H_0 \mid \text{data})\): that needs a prior.

The power of a test is…

Power \(= P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ false}) = 1 - \beta\), where \(\beta\) is the Type II (missed effect) rate.

You run 20 independent tests at \(\alpha = 0.05\) and every null is true. What's the probability of at least one false positive? (Exact form or a decimal.)

\(1 - 0.95^{20} \approx 0.64\). This is why multiple-comparison corrections (Bonferroni, Holm, FDR) exist.

7 · Regression

In simple linear regression of \(y\) on \(x\): correlation \(r = 0.6\), \(s_y = 10\), \(s_x = 2\). What is the least-squares slope?

\(\hat\beta_1 = r \, s_y / s_x = 0.6 \cdot 10 / 2 = 3\).

Same regression. What is \(R^2\)?

In simple regression \(R^2 = r^2 = 0.36\): 36% of the variance in \(y\) is explained by the line.

Across months, ice-cream sales and drownings are strongly correlated. In two or three sentences: why can't you conclude ice cream causes drowning, and what would you check or do to get at causation?

Full credit: names a confounder (heat/summer → more swimming and more ice cream); proposes controlling for it (stratify/regress on temperature or season) or, ideally, a randomized experiment / natural experiment. Partial: says "correlation isn't causation" without a mechanism or a remedy.
Warm weather drives both: more people swim (more drownings) and more people buy ice cream. Adjust for temperature or season, or use a design that breaks the confounding (randomization, a natural experiment).

8 · Bayesian basics

Prior on a success rate: \(\text{Beta}(1,1)\) (uniform). You observe 7 successes in 10 trials. What is the posterior mean?

Beta–binomial conjugacy: posterior \(\text{Beta}(1+7,\ 1+3)\), mean \(\frac{8}{12} = \frac23\). The prior acts like one extra success and one extra failure.

9 · Statistics for ML and experiments

Making a model more flexible (more parameters, less regularization) typically…

More flexibility fits the training data more closely (lower bias) but makes the fit swing more from sample to sample (higher variance). The best test error sits in between.

What does random assignment buy you in an experiment (e.g., an A/B test)?

Randomization makes treatment independent of every pre-existing factor, measured or not, so on average the groups differ only by treatment. That's what licenses a causal reading.
