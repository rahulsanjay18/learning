Course description

The theory of point estimation at graduate level, from Lehmann and Casella's Theory of Point Estimation. It covers exponential families and sufficiency in full generality, unbiasedness and the information inequality, equivariance, Bayes and empirical Bayes estimation (including James–Stein), minimaxity and admissibility, and asymptotic optimality. S302 gave you the undergraduate version of most of these results. This course proves them under weaker assumptions and shows where they break.

Learning objectives

By the end of this course you can:

  1. Use completeness and sufficiency to find uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimators in exponential families.
  2. Prove the information inequality in its general form, and identify when it can be attained.
  3. Find minimum risk equivariant estimators for location and scale problems.
  4. Derive Bayes and empirical Bayes estimators, and prove that the James–Stein estimator dominates the sample mean in three or more dimensions.
  5. Prove an estimator is minimax or admissible using the standard techniques (least favorable priors, Blyth's method).
  6. Prove the asymptotic efficiency of the maximum likelihood estimator under standard conditions.

Texts

  • Required: Lehmann and Casella, Theory of Point Estimation, 2nd ed. (Springer, 1998; to buy): Chapter 1 "Preparations", Chapter 2 "Unbiasedness", Chapter 3 "Equivariance", Chapter 4 "Average Risk Optimality", Chapter 5 "Minimaxity and Admissibility", Chapter 6 "Asymptotic Optimality".
  • Recommended: Keener, Theoretical Statistics: Topics for a Core Course (Springer, 2010; to buy), as a gentler second explanation.
  • Recommended: Efron and Hastie, Computer Age Statistical Inference, Chapter 7 "James–Stein Estimation and Ridge Regression".
