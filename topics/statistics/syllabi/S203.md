## Course description
Chapter 3 of Casella and Berger: the standard families of distributions. For each one we cover where it comes from and how the
families are related. Then come exponential families, location and scale families, and a few inequalities and identities the
book uses for the rest of the way. Exponential families matter most here. They are the reason sufficient statistics, maximum
likelihood and generalized linear models work as neatly as they do.

At placement you were rusty on the geometric and Poisson distributions and on memorylessness, so the pretest will check those directly.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this course you can:
1. Give the formula, support, mean, variance and mgf of each common family, and describe a situation that produces it.
2. Derive the relationships between families, such as the Poisson limit of the binomial and the gamma–exponential–chi-squared links.
3. Prove that memorylessness characterizes the geometric and exponential distributions.
4. Recognize an exponential family, write it in canonical form, and use it to find the mean and variance of its sufficient statistic.
5. Build a location–scale family from a standard density and find its moments.
6. Prove Chebychev's inequality and use Stein's lemma.

## Texts
- **Required:** Casella and Berger, 2nd ed., Chapter 3, "Common Families of Distributions", pp. 85–127, and the table of common distributions at the back of the book. In your library.
- **Recommended:** Blitzstein and Hwang, *Introduction to Probability*, 2nd ed. (free from the authors), Chapters 3–5, for the story behind each discrete distribution.
- **Optional:** Leemis and McQueston, "Univariate Distribution Relationships", *The American Statistician*, 2008 (free online). It's a one-page chart of how the families connect.
