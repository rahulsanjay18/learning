## Course description
Chapter 8 of Casella and Berger: hypothesis testing. We cover likelihood ratio tests, Bayesian tests, union–intersection tests,
error probabilities and the power function, the Neyman–Pearson lemma and uniformly most powerful tests, p-values, and loss
functions. This course also includes the three lessons left over from S150. Those are likelihood ratios with the A/B-test example
(where power turns out to be about 55%), multiple testing, and a complete A/B test from design to conclusion. Last, there's a
short section on why randomization removes confounding, which your placement answer showed you needed.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this course you can:
1. Construct a likelihood ratio test and simplify it to a test based on a sufficient statistic.
2. Compute a power function and choose a sample size that gives the power you want.
3. Prove the Neyman–Pearson lemma, and use the Karlin–Rubin theorem to find a uniformly most powerful test.
4. Define a p-value as a statistic, prove it is valid, and explain why a small p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.
5. Control the chance of any false positive across k tests with Bonferroni, and spot hidden multiple testing in an analysis (many metrics, repeated peeking).
6. Design, run and write up an A/B test: sample size from power, the test, the interval, and the conclusion in plain words.
7. Explain why random assignment removes confounding when adjusting for observed variables doesn't.

## Notes
The exercises at the end of the chapter (pp. 402–413) are optional homework. Watch the notation: Casella and Berger write β(θ) for the power function, while most applied books use β for the probability of a miss. Each lesson points this out where it comes up.
