Is it safe?

Counting one square to know whether a capture wins or loses material. About 25 minutes.

Why this comes first

You want to understand chess, not just win. But below about 1200, most games are decided by material given away for nothing, and no plan survives a lost piece. Seirawan puts it this way: tactics are “the guard dogs of strategy”1. Dan Heisman calls the habit of moving without checking the opponent's forcing replies (checks, captures, threats) Hope Chess2. The opposite habit, checking that each move is safe before you play it, starts with one skill: counting a single square.

Today's win: given a capture, you can say who comes out ahead in material, and why.

1 · Warm-up: recall the point count

From memory, no peeking. Getting it wrong here is useful too: struggling to recall is what makes it stick.

A knight is worth how many pawns?

Knight = 3, the same as a bishop.1

A rook is worth how many pawns?

Rook = 5. Trading a minor piece (3) for a rook is called “winning the Exchange”, a 2-point gain.1

A queen is worth how many pawns?

Queen = 9. The king is “priceless”: it is never traded.1

2 · The idea: keep a running tally

A capture starts an exchange on one square. Two facts drive it:

  1. Cheapest first. Each side recaptures with its least valuable piece that can reach the square, so it risks as little as possible.
  2. Anyone can stop. Nobody has to recapture. Each side keeps going only while that helps it.

So you don't just count heads. You write down the score after each capture and ask, at each step, “would the side to move rather stop here?”

Worked example

The pawn on e5 has two attackers (knight f3, bishop c3) and two defenders (knight c6, queen e7). The heads are equal, so it looks even. Now run the tally from White's side:

MoveWhat changed handsWhite's running score
1. Nxe5White takes a pawn+1
1… Nxe5Black takes a knight−2
2. Bxe5White takes a knight+1
2… Qxe5Black takes a bishop−2

White ends at −2 whether it stops after move 1 or plays it all out. The problem is the very first trade: a 3-point knight for a 1-point pawn that is defended. Equal numbers are not enough; the values decide.

The two-question shortcut. A piece is safe where it stands if:

  1. no enemy piece worth less attacks it, and
  2. it has at least as many defenders as attackers.

If either answer is “no”, run the tally. The tally is the real answer; the shortcut only saves time. Heisman teaches the same count of attackers vs. defenders, and the cases where it doesn't apply3.

3 · Practice

Each position: White plays the arrowed capture. After the best play by both sides on that square (either side may stop), who ends up ahead? Every answer was checked by a script that plays out the exchange6.

The knight on e5 is defended by a pawn. Who comes out ahead?

dxe5 dxe5: White gives a pawn (1) for a knight (3), so +2. The knight was attacked by something cheaper, so its defender doesn't matter. That's question 1 of the shortcut.

Rook takes rook. Who comes out ahead?

Rxd8 Rxd8: a rook for a rook, 0. Equal value, one attacker and one defender: an even trade. Whether you want to trade is a strategy question for later.

Three attackers on d5 (knight, bishop, queen) against two defenders (pawn, knight). Who comes out ahead?

Nxd5 exd5 Bxd5: +1, −2, −1. Black can stop there, or play Nxd5 Qxd5 (still −1). White got two pawns for a knight: −1. More attackers didn't help, because Black's first defender was a cheap pawn.

Compare this with the worked example: one Black piece is missing. Who comes out ahead?

Two attackers, one defender. Nxe5 wins a pawn, +1. If Black recaptures with Nxe5, then Bxe5 and White is still +1. Recapturing just trades knights, so Black might as well not bother. Without the queen on e7, the pawn hangs.

One attacker, one defender. Who comes out ahead?

Nxe5 dxe5: a knight (3) for a pawn (1), −2. Equal counts, but the defender is cheaper than the attacker. This is the classic way to hang a knight below 1200.

Black's knight on d4 is defended by the other knight and the queen. White attacks it three times. Who comes out ahead?

Nxd4 +3. If Nxd4 Bxd4, White is still +3, and now Black's last defender is the queen: Qxd4? Rxd4 loses 9 points for 3. So Black stops, and White wins a knight: +3. A defender that is too valuable to recapture doesn't really count.

Knight takes knight. Who comes out ahead?

Nxe5 Nxe5: a knight for a knight, 0.

4 · Where counting stops working

The tally assumes nothing else on the board matters. Seirawan shows a position where Black is a queen up on points but loses it at once to a discovered check, and says: “instead of counting points, look carefully at the position.”1 Checks, pins and double attacks break the count. They are the next layer, and they are why Heisman's routine starts with checks, then captures, then threats2.

5 · Take it to a real game

Play one untimed game against a chess.com bot around 1000–1200, with hints and takebacks off. Before every move, ask one question:

“After this move, can my opponent capture anything of mine and come out ahead?” Use the two-question shortcut on whatever your move leaves behind.

Then paste the PGN to your teacher (Share → PGN). Mark any move where you forgot to ask. That gap is the habit we're building.
