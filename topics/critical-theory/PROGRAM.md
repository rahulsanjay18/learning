# Critical Theory: the major (designed, parked)

*Designed 2026-10-08 at the learner's request ("not one I would do now"). Parked in `programs.json` with a full curriculum, so
it's ready when a slot opens (one in, one out). Shape follows `notes/major-design.md`. The professor's syllabus, week by week, is
`SYLLABUS.md`. Every reading with its length and day count is in `READING-PLAN.md`. Both are generated by `build_plan.py` and
`build_syllabus.py`.*

## TL;DR
- **What it is:** twelve lenses for reading any text (New Criticism → ecocriticism), plus the thinkers they stand on (Hegel,
  Nietzsche, Marx, Weber, Durkheim, Freud, Saussure), plus the Frankfurt School as its own course.
- **How it's taught:** this is a **reading major**, a different kind of program from Statistics or Games. Most days you read
  one ~25-minute slice with 2–3 guide questions. Every fifth day is a seminar (a real lesson page). Each unit ends with a short lens essay.
- **Three books per lens:** Tyson's chapter (what the lens is, with a model reading of *Gatsby*), the theorists in their own words
  (the *Norton Anthology of Theory and Criticism*), and a novel or play to apply it to. All of them are in your library at grade A.
- **Size:** Level I is 474 reading days + 128 seminar/essay days ≈ **128 weeks at 5 sittings a week** (≈ 20 months if you read
  daily; ≈ 6 years at a half subject's 2 blocks a week). Computed in `READING-PLAN.md`. Level II (whole primary books) is opt-in.

## 1. Plan
**Why (draft; correct it in MISSION.md):** you asked for the lenses by which a text can be analyzed, the factual and theoretical
background of each, and books to apply them to, with the Frankfurt School as background. It's a "good for the soul" major. It
also connects to two active majors: Indian History (Yashpal, Manto, Chughtai, Rushdie; postcolonial theory) and Games
(culture industry, film and game theory; elective CT350).

**Two meanings of "critical theory".** Capitalized, *Critical Theory* is the Frankfurt School's program: Horkheimer, Adorno,
Marcuse, Benjamin, Habermas (Bronner VSI, ch. 1 "The Frankfurt School"; Held). Lowercase, *critical theory* is the umbrella term
literature departments use for all the lenses (Tyson's usage). This major covers both. CT260 is the capital-C course.

**Your point (2026-10-08): "you'd mostly just be assigning me readings."** Agreed, and the design leans into it rather than
dressing readings up as lessons:
- **Reading days are the course.** One slice, a reading guide, nothing to submit. Answering the guide in your head counts.
- **Seminars do what a lecture and discussion section would.** They recap the week, explain the hard passages (Derrida and
  Adorno need it), add a few recall questions to daily review, and ask one discussion question for a ~150–200-word answer.
- **The lens essay is the exam.** It's the only place I grade mastery seriously (TEACHING-LOG rule 20).

**Your other point: it has to draw on all the humanities.** It does. The lens chapters are literary, but their backgrounds come from:

That mix is what general-education cores teach: Columbia's *Contemporary Civilization* requires Marx, Freud, Du Bois and
Fanon [4]. Chicago's *Self, Culture and Society* runs from Smith, Marx and Weber through Durkheim to Freud, Marcuse, Beauvoir
and Fanon [5]. Yale's ENGL 300 covers the literary side, from formalism to queer theory [3]. This major covers both halves.

**Shape.** The sequence follows Tyson's chapters, with two deviations (my judgment):
1. **New Criticism comes first, not fourth.** Close reading is the skill every lens uses, including the lenses that attack it.
   Tyson says the chapters can be read "in any order" [1].
2. **Each foundations course comes just in time,** right before the lenses that need it, so you aren't reading 19 weeks of
   philosophy and sociology before the first lens.

**Novels come back.** *Gatsby* (Tyson reads it in every chapter), *Frankenstein* (structuralism, then deconstruction, then
ecocriticism), *Heart of Darkness* (new historicism, then queer and postcolonial criticism, then the CT290 capstone with its
published critics) and *To the Lighthouse* each return under a new lens. This cuts reading time, and seeing the same text change
under different lenses is the whole point.

## 3. A day, a week, a unit
- **Reading day (Mon–Thu).** One slice sized to ~25 minutes. Sizes are my judgment: ~6,000 words of easy prose (Tyson, novels),
  ~5,000 of a VSI, ~3,500 of dense theory (≈ 5 Norton pages, ≈ 9 of Held), ~4,000 of Shakespeare. Lengths come from your
  library's word counts and each book's contents pages (`build_plan.py`). The guide questions get written when the course
  starts (rule 12: three questions + "what surprised or confused you").
- **Seminar (Fri).** A lesson page from Markdown (`render_lesson.py`): recap boxes (rule 29), the week's hardest idea explained,
  3–5 recall questions that enter review, and the seminar question as a graded `free` response.
- **Lens essay (the unit's last Friday).** 600–800 words (1,500 for CT290), graded with the rubric in SYLLABUS.md.
- **How optional is what** (you asked me to decide): reading = the course. Guide questions = optional. Seminar recall = do it,
  it feeds review. Seminar paragraph = expected but skippable, since the essay can carry the objective. Lens essay = the one real
  requirement, because without it there's no evidence for "Got it".
- **Pretest.** CT000 is one sitting. A pass skips CT100's Culler half. Each lens course also opens with a 3-question check (name
  the lens's core concepts), and if you know them, the Tyson chapter becomes a skim.
