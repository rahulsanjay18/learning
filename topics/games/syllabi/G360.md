## Course description
An optional course on politics and diplomacy modeled as games. It covers why wars happen even though fighting is costly (the
bargaining model), how domestic politics constrains negotiators (two-level games), how threats and commitments work (Schelling),
and reputation in repeated play. It ends with *Diplomacy*, a board game with no dice, and Meta's CICERO, the AI that plays it by
negotiating in natural language.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this course you can:
1. Explain Fearon's three rationalist explanations for war: private information, commitment problems and indivisibility.
2. Explain two-level games and win-sets, with an example.
3. Explain Schelling's ideas of commitment, compellence and the manipulation of risk.
4. Use repeated-game reasoning to explain reputation.
5. Play a game of *Diplomacy* and analyse it with these ideas.

## Texts
- **Required (in your library):** Schelling, *Arms and Influence* (Yale, 2008 edition), Chapters 1 "The Diplomacy of Violence", 2 "The Art of Commitment", 3 "The Manipulation of Risk".
- **Required (free articles):** James Fearon, "Rationalist Explanations for War", *International Organization* 49:3 (1995). Robert Putnam, "Diplomacy and Domestic Politics: The Logic of Two-Level Games", *International Organization* 42:3 (1988). Meta FAIR, "Human-level play in the game of Diplomacy by combining language models with strategic reasoning", *Science* 378 (2022).
- **Recommended (in your library):** Binmore, *Game Theory: A Very Short Introduction*, Chapter 5 "Reciprocity".
