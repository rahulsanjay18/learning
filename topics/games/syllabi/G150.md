## Course description
An introduction to military strategy, studied as a system for making decisions under uncertainty. The main text is Echevarria's
*Military Strategy: A Very Short Introduction*. It sorts strategies by what they target (the enemy's capacity or will) and how
(fast or slow). We add two classic texts, Clausewitz and Sun Tzu, and finish with a campaign analysis from Polybius's account of
Hannibal. The concepts here become game mechanics later: fog of war becomes hidden information, supply becomes resource networks,
and deterrence becomes game theory.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this course you can:
1. Classify a strategy as annihilation, dislocation, attrition or exhaustion, and explain the choice.
2. Tell deterrence from coercion, and explain why a threat has to be credible.
3. Explain Clausewitz's "war is the continuation of policy by other means", friction, and the trinity.
4. Compare Sun Tzu and Clausewitz on at least one point.
5. Explain why decapitation and terror usually fail to reach their political aims.
6. Analyse a campaign by its aims, methods and means, and explain why it succeeded or failed.

## Texts
- **Required:** Echevarria, *Military Strategy: A Very Short Introduction* (in your library): Chapters 1 "What is military strategy?", 2 "Annihilation and dislocation", 3 "Attrition and exhaustion", 4 "Deterrence and coercion", 5 "Terror and terrorism", 6 "Decapitation and targeted killing", 8 "What causes military strategies to succeed or fail?".
- **Required (in your library):** Clausewitz, *On War*, Book 1, Chapter 1. Sun Tzu, *The Art of War* (Giles's translation), Chapters I–III. Polybius, *The Rise of the Roman Empire* (Penguin), Book III.
- **Optional (in your library):** Howard, *Clausewitz: A Very Short Introduction*; Freedman, *Strategy: A History*; Echevarria Ch. 7 "Cyber power and military strategy".
