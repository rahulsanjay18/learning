## Course description
Games with dice. They add one new ingredient, chance, while everything else stays visible. We start with Pig, a simple dice game
that teaches expected value and when to stop. Then comes a backgammon unit: the pip count, the doubling cube (when to double, when
to take), and how a computer handles chance with expectimax. The course ends with TD-Gammon, the 1990s program that learned
backgammon by playing itself and pointed the way to AlphaZero.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this course you can:
1. Compute expected values and find the optimal stopping rule in Pig.
2. Play backgammon competently: opening moves, the pip count, priming and hitting.
3. Decide when to double and when to take, using the take point (about 25% winning chances, ignoring gammons and cube ownership).
4. Explain expectimax and why chance nodes make search harder.
5. Explain how TD-Gammon learned by self-play with temporal-difference learning.

## Texts
- **Required:** Paul Magriel, *Backgammon* (1976; to buy), the chapters on the opening, the pip count, and the doubling cube.
- **Recommended (in your library):** Haigh, *Probability: A Very Short Introduction*, for expected value.
- **Free:** Sutton and Barto, *Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction*, 2nd ed., §16.1 "TD-Gammon". Neller and Presser, "Optimal Play of the Dice Game Pig", *The UMAP Journal* 25:1 (2004).
