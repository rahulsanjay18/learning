# How to do an ends–ways–means breakdown

A step-by-step method for the "Take it outside" exercise in [Lesson 1](../lessons/0001-capacity-or-will.html).
Sources are numbered at the bottom. Where a step is my own synthesis rather than a source's claim, it says so.

---

## The one-sentence version

For each side, write down **what it wanted** (end), **how it expected fighting to get it there** (way),
and **what it had to fight with** (means). Then find the **assumption** linking them and ask whether that assumption held. [1]

## Before you start: pick a scope

- **One war, one phase.** Echevarria notes that strategy is usually practiced by "dividing the conflict into phases or campaigns" [1, Ch. 1].
  Sides often change strategy between phases (e.g. Carthage after Cannae), so analyze one phase at a time.
- **Both sides.** Strategy only exists when there's an adversary: "If the environment is competitive and an adversary is present, one needs a strategy; otherwise, a plan will suffice" [1, Ch. 1].
  A breakdown of one side alone is a plan review, not a strategy analysis.
- **Get the history first.** Read a short account before you analyze. The Oxford VSIs in your collection
  (*Ancient Warfare*, *The Napoleonic Wars*, *The First World War*, *World War II*, *The Cold War*) are good for this.

## The steps (do them for each side)

### 1. End: what did they want politically?
- Write the **political** aim, not the military one. "Destroy the Roman army" is a military objective; "make Rome pay an indemnity and give back Sicily" is the end.
- A useful test: **what terms would have made them stop?**
- Military objectives are means to political ends. Clausewitz says to evaluate each objective as a means to the next one up,
  "until we reach one that requires no justification", which ultimately "is to bring about peace" [2, Bk 2 Ch 5].

### 2. Way: how did they expect to get there?
- Name the **cell** from lesson 1: annihilation, dislocation, attrition or exhaustion (or a later type such as deterrence or coercion).
  Echevarria: "Ways are essentially types of military strategy, or combinations of them" [1, Ch. 1].
- Then write **the bet**: the assumption about the *enemy* that makes this way produce the end.
  For example: "if we destroy their army, they will negotiate."
  *(Calling this "the bet" is my framing. The idea comes from Echevarria's verdict that Hannibal's strategy "rested on an incorrect assumption about his opponent" [1, Ch. 2].)*

### 3. Means: what did they have to do it with?
- Military power **for this situation**. Echevarria defines it as "the ability to perform specific combat missions in a given situation" [1, Ch. 1]:
  submarines don't help in counterinsurgency, and a well-equipped but untrained militia isn't much power.
- Include the non-military instruments (diplomatic, informational, economic) and **allies** [1, Ch. 1].
- Since tactics and weapons are in scope for you, note any **technology or tactical edge**, e.g. modern US ships at Manila Bay [1, Ch. 2].

### 4. Risk: do the three fit, and what if the bet fails?
- **Balance:** are the means enough to reach the end through this way? There's "no scientific method" for "how much is enough"; it's judgment [1, Ch. 1].
- **Fallback:** if the bet is wrong, is there a plan B?
- **Whose risk?** A commander sees risk as the chance the mission fails; a head of state sees it as political capital spent [1, Ch. 1].
- For slow strategies (attrition, exhaustion), Echevarria lists three checks [1, Ch. 3]:
  (a) your resolve vs. the enemy's, given the stakes; (b) whether material strength alone could be decisive; (c) how physical and political geography will help or hurt.

### 5. Put the two sides against each other
- Does each side's way attack the other's **weakness** or its **strength**? The strategist's task is "countering the strengths and exploiting the weaknesses of an opponent" [1, Ch. 1].
- A common pattern: one side bets on **capacity** and the other on **will** (Vietnam, Hannibal vs. Rome).

### 6. Verdict: where did the chain break (or hold)?
Walk the chain and find the weakest link:

| Link | Failure looks like |
|---|---|
| End | Unrealistic or confused aim (e.g. Carthage's divided grand strategy [1, Ch. 2]) |
| Way → End | The bet about the enemy was wrong (Cannae) |
| Means → Way | Not enough of the right kind of power |
| Interaction | The enemy adapted: a quick war became a slow one |

Clausewitz's warnings for this step [2, Bk 2 Ch 5]:
- "effects in war seldom result from a single cause". Name more than one cause.
- Theory's principles are "aids to judgment", never laws. Don't condemn a choice just because it breaks a rule. Ask why the commander made it.

## Template (copy this)

```
War / phase:            ______________________  (dates)

SIDE A: ____________
  End:    (political aim; what terms would make them stop?)
  Way:    (cell: annihilation / dislocation / attrition / exhaustion / other)
          The bet: "If we ___, then the enemy will ___."
  Means:  (forces, technology/tactical edge, allies, economy, information)
  Risk:   (do the three fit? plan B if the bet fails?)

SIDE B: ____________
  End:
  Way:    The bet:
  Means:
  Risk:

Interaction: whose way hit the other's weakness?
Verdict:     which link held or broke, and why? (name 2+ causes)
```

For lesson 1's homework, just the **End / Way / Means** lines for each side is enough. Add the rest when you can.

## Worked example: Hannibal vs. Rome, 218–216 BCE (up to Cannae)

All facts here are from Echevarria, Ch. 1–3 [1].

**Carthage (Hannibal)**
- **End:** not to destroy Rome but to reduce its power and restore the prestige Carthage lost in the First Punic War.
  Historians think an **indemnity** and the return of **Corsica and Sicily** would have satisfied him.
- **Way:** **annihilation**. Destroy Roman armies in the field, as at Cannae (double envelopment).
  *The bet:* "If we inflict a defeat this big, Rome will negotiate, the way defeated states traditionally did."
- **Means:** about 50,000 men at Cannae against about 80,000 Romans. A veteran African heavy infantry, strong cavalry, and Hannibal's own generalship.
- **Risk:** no appealing alternative if Rome refused to negotiate. Back home, Carthage's grand strategy was split between landed and maritime interests.

**Rome**
- **End:** survive and avenge its losses. The Senate **voted not to negotiate** after Cannae.
- **Way:** after Cannae, a long struggle for allies. Earlier, Fabius had used **exhaustion**, avoiding battle and "hitting the enemy army in the stomach".
- **Means:** the ability to keep raising new legions, despite losing about 50,000 dead and 20,000 captured at Cannae.

**Interaction:** Hannibal bet on Roman **will** breaking after a **capacity** blow. Rome's will held, so the war became slow, the kind of war Rome could sustain.

**Verdict:** the **Way → End** link broke. The bet about the enemy was wrong; Echevarria says the error lay "in expecting his enemy to act in a certain way".
It's not a clean failure, though. Cannae brought Greek colonies and Italian cities over to Hannibal and gave him "rough strategic parity" for the next phase.
Echevarria puts the final defeat down more to Carthage's divided grand strategy (an **End** problem). That's two causes, as Clausewitz asks.

## AI / decision-theory mapping (my analogy, not from the sources)

| Strategy term | Rough AI equivalent |
|---|---|
| End | Objective / utility function (and when the opponent will settle = the terminal condition) |
| Way | Policy |
| Means | Resources and action space |
| The bet | Your **model of the opponent**. Cannae is a policy that was optimal under a wrong opponent model. |
| Risk / no plan B | Not hedging against model error, i.e. no robustness to the opponent's type |

This is why a wargame or multi-agent sim lives or dies on how it models the *opponent's will*, not just unit strengths.
