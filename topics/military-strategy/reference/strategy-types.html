Types of military strategy

A one-page sheet for analyzing any campaign. Everything here follows Echevarria, Military Strategy: A Very Short Introduction, Ch. 1–3, unless marked otherwise.

Definition

Military strategy is the practice of reducing an adversary's physical capacity and willingness to fight, and continuing to do so until one's aim is achieved.

If there's an adversary in a competitive environment, you need a strategy. If not, a plan will do.

The four basic types

CapacityWill
Quick AnnihilationDestroy forces in one or two big battles, often by encirclement. Ulm 1805, Manila Bay 1898, Cannae 216 BCE. DislocationUnexpected maneuver or surprise that unbalances the enemy psychologically. France 1940.
Slow AttritionDestroy forces faster than they're replaced while your own losses stay bearable. Allies in WWII. ExhaustionWear down willingness: make the war seem endless or unwinnable. Fabius, Washington, Ho Chi Minh, blockades.

Each pair blurs in practice: classify by the main mechanism. Quick strategies that don't break the enemy's will turn into slow ones (Cannae). Attrition favors the materially stronger side. Exhaustion lets a weaker side outlast a stronger one, and it suits the defense.

The other types (later lessons)

DeterrenceDissuade an opponent from doing something.
CoercionCompel an opponent to do something. Usually paired with deterrence.
Terror / terrorismUse fear to change behavior: strategic bombing of vital centers, or attacks on noncombatants.
DecapitationParalyze a group by removing its leadership. A child of dislocation.
Targeted killingSystematically eliminate an organization's members. A child of attrition.
CyberNot a type of its own: cyber power changes the means and so what ways are possible.

Analysis frame: ends + ways + means (+ risk)

From Arthur Lykke (1989). Ends = objectives. Ways = courses of action, i.e. strategy types. Means = military power. A “balanced” strategy has means sufficient to reach the ends by the chosen ways, but there's no formula for “enough”. Risk means different things to a commander (chance the mission fails) and to a head of state (political capital spent).

Example: NATO in central Europe. End: deter a Warsaw Pact attack. Way: strong defensive posture. Means: nuclear, conventional and special forces.

Levels

Grand strategy is the head of state's business: alliances, treaties, all instruments of power (diplomatic, informational, military, economic). Military strategy is the general's business, ideally nested inside grand strategy, though in practice it sometimes drives it. Below that come campaigns, battles and tactics. Clausewitz: tactics is the use of forces to win engagements; strategy is the use of engagements to achieve the purpose of the war.

The nine principles of war (as commonly listed)

Objective · Maneuver · Surprise · Mass · Economy of force · Offensive · Security · Simplicity · Unity of command. Echevarria warns they are “not necessarily” timeless or universal: how much each helps depends on the situation.

Questions to ask of any campaign

  1. What was each side's political end?
  2. Which cell was each side counting on: capacity or will, quick or slow?
  3. Did the means match? Who had the material edge, and who had the greater resolve given the stakes?
  4. Did a quick strategy fail and become a slow one? Why?
  5. How did geography and technology shape what was possible?

Source: Antulio J. Echevarria II, Military Strategy: A Very Short Introduction (Oxford University Press), Ch. 1–3, in your collection. Lykke via War Room, US Army War College.
