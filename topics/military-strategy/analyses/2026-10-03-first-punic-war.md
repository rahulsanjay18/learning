## Your analysis (as submitted)

## What you got right

- **The core asymmetry.** Carthage was stronger at sea and Rome on land. Polybius says the same in his comparison of the two states:
  the Carthaginians were "better trained and equipped" at sea, "but in military campaigns the Romans train themselves to an altogether higher standard" [1, 6.52].
- **The mercenary point is a real strategic insight**, not just a detail. Polybius makes it the *reason* Rome could outlast Carthage:
  Carthage "employ[s] foreign and mercenary troops", Rome uses citizens and allies, so "even if they happen to be defeated at the outset, the Romans carry on the war with all their resources, but this is impossible for the Carthaginians" [1, 6.52].
  That's a *means* fact feeding the *will* axis. Good catch.
- **The corvus** ("raven" in this translation): correct. Polybius says it was suggested because Roman ships were "poorly fitted-out and difficult to manoeuvre" [1, 1.22], and Carthage had "the faster vessels" [1, 1.26].
  It turned a sea battle into a land battle, playing to Rome's strength. That's a tactical device serving strategy.
- **"It turned into a grind that Rome won"** is the right instinct. Polybius calls it "the longest, the most continuous and the greatest war of which we have knowledge": 24 years [1, 1.63].

## What to sharpen

### 1. End: be specific, and notice that Rome's end *changed*
"Expand" is true of both sides but too vague to analyze: a strategy needs an end you could check off.
- **How it started:** Messana, a city held by mercenaries called the Mamertines, asked Rome for help. The Senate hesitated; the people voted for war, partly because the consuls promised "great gains … from the spoils of war" [1, 1.11].
  Rome's first end was small: relieve Messana.
- **How it escalated:** after taking Agrigentum (261), the Senate's "aspirations soared far above their original designs". They now aimed "to drive the Carthaginians out of Sicily altogether" [1, 1.20].
- **How it ended:** Carthage evacuated all of Sicily and the islands between Sicily and Italy, and paid 3,200 talents (the Roman people raised the first draft's 2,200) [1, 1.62–63]. Rome got its escalated end.
- **Carthage's end:** Polybius doesn't state it in the passages I read. A reasonable *inference* is that Carthage was defending its existing position in Sicily and its control of the sea. Mark it as an inference in your write-up.

**Lesson:** ends shift mid-war, often after a success. That is itself something to analyze.

### 2. Way: "seizing land" isn't annihilation, and the way *did* shift
- **Annihilation** means destroying the enemy's *forces* in one or two battles [2, Ch. 1–2]. Taking towns and territory over years is a different thing.
  Echevarria lists **sieges** among the forms of **exhaustion** [2, Ch. 3]. A 24-year war is in the *slow* row by definition.
- What you noticed is real, though: both sides fought *battles* meant to destroy the other's force. Echevarria has a name for this nesting:
  "we might design a battle of encirclement and annihilation as part of a campaign of dislocation, which, in turn, might contribute to an overall strategy of attrition or exhaustion" [2, Ch. 1].
  So the better phrasing is: **annihilation-style battles inside an attrition war.** Your instinct, made precise.
- **Rome's way shifted at least four times** (all Polybius, Book I):
  1. **Land war in Sicily** (264–261).
  2. **Build a navy** "because they saw that the war was dragging on": 100 quinqueremes, with crews taught to row on land [1, 1.20–21]. Coastal cities kept defecting to whichever side controlled the sea [1, 1.20].
  3. **A quick knockout: invade Africa** (256–255). Regulus "counted on taking the city [Carthage] in a very short time" and offered terms so harsh Carthage refused [1, 1.31].
     Then the Spartan mercenary Xanthippus used elephants and cavalry to destroy Regulus's army; only about 2,000 Romans survived [1, 1.32–34].
     *That* was the annihilation bet, and it failed.
  4. **After fleets were lost to storms, abandon the sea.** For five years Rome fought by land alone [1, 1.39, 1.59].
     Then a third fleet, funded by private citizens when "there were no funds in the treasury", won at the Aegates Islands (Polybius: Aegusa) in 241 [1, 1.59–61].
- **Carthage's Hamilcar Barca** fought in Sicily with "ambushes … sorties and counter-attacks" that could not "be brought to a decisive issue" [1, 1.57].
  Avoiding a decisive battle and raiding is how Echevarria describes **exhaustion** [2, Ch. 3].

### 3. How the war actually ended: capacity, not will
This part is subtle, and it's a 2×2 question:
- After the Aegates defeat, Carthage, "so far as resolution and the will to conquer were concerned, [was] still ready to fight on, but when it came to calculating their resources, they found themselves in an impasse".
  Rome controlled the sea, so Carthage couldn't supply its army in Sicily [1, 1.62].
- So Carthage's **capacity** broke before its **will** did. That's **attrition** winning.
- But Rome didn't win by losing less: it lost about **700 quinqueremes to Carthage's 500**, counting shipwrecks [1, 1.63], and Polybius says Rome was "worn out and exhausted" by the end [1, 1.62].
  Rome won because it could **replace** its losses (citizen manpower, allies, private money, and copying a captured Carthaginian ship design [1, 1.59]) and kept the will to do it.
  That's Echevarria's definition of attrition: destroy forces "faster than they can be replaced, while … ensuring one's own rate of loss remains bearable" [2, Ch. 3].

### 4. Means: small fixes
- **"2 legions."** Polybius describes Rome's standard army as **four legions**, two per consul, plus allied contingents [1, Book VI, the levy]. Two legions would be one consul's army.
- **The biggest Roman means weren't weapons.** They were manpower (citizens plus Italian allies), the ability to rebuild fleets fast, and money. These, not swords, decided an attrition war.
- **Carthage:** you have the navy and elephants right. Add **wealth** (it hired mercenaries) and **commanders** (Xanthippus, Hamilcar), who repeatedly did more with less.
- "Five-oared boats": these are *quinqueremes* ("fives"). I couldn't confirm in your sources what the "five" counts, so don't rely on "five oars".

Then the **verdict**: which link held or broke?

## Suggested verdict (compare with yours before reading)

<details><summary>Click to reveal</summary>

Rome won because it (a) **changed its means to fit the theater that decided the war**: sea control decided the coastal cities and supply in Sicily;
and (b) could **replace losses faster and keep the will to do so** through citizen manpower, allies and private funding [1, 1.20, 1.59, 6.52].
Carthage's bet on its naval edge and on Hamilcar wearing Rome down in Sicily failed when one sea battle cut its army's supply [1, 1.62].
Rome's own quick-knockout bet (Regulus in Africa) failed badly, so even the winner's chain broke at least once. Two causes, as Clausewitz asks.
