## Why this lesson
Your First Punic War analysis called both sides' way "annihilation", because both were "aggressively seizing land". That's the
most natural mistake there is: annihilation *sounds* like the aggressive strategy. This lesson takes the two axes apart again, so
that "who is attacking?" and "which strategy?" become separate questions.

::: free read-cannae
Cannae was a complete annihilation *battle*. Why didn't it win Hannibal the *war*? Use Echevarria's term for his mistake.
--- rubric
Rome didn't negotiate: the Senate voted not to, and kept raising legions. Hannibal assumed Rome would act the way defeated states
traditionally did: the "Hannibalic fallacy", a wrong assumption about the opponent. Bonus: Cannae still improved his position
(allies defected), and the war moved into a protracted phase.

only defenders: blockades and sieges are forms of exhaustion an attacker uses (the chapter lists them). Credit for "suits" ≠ "only".

## Block 2: the lesson

### The two axes, again (from the reading)
Start from the definitions, which have no conditions attached [1]:
- **Annihilation** aims to win quickly "by severely reducing or eliminating an opponent's material strength through one or two
  major battles" (Ch. 2).
- **Attrition** means "destroying an opponent's forces faster than they can be replaced, while at the same time ensuring one's own
  rate of loss remains bearable" (Ch. 3).
- **Exhaustion** is "wearing down a foe's willingness to fight", "a form of psychological attrition" (Ch. 3).

Read the definitions again and look for the words *attack*, *offensive*, *defend*. They aren't there. Every definition answers
only two questions: **what is being destroyed** (material strength, or willingness to fight) and **how fast** (one or two battles,
or a grind over time). Who is attacking isn't one of the axes.

::: choice axes-offense
So what can you conclude about an army that is on the offensive, advancing and taking territory?
- [ ] Its strategy must be annihilation
- [ ] Its strategy must be attrition
- [x] Its strategy could be anything
- [ ] Its strategy must be exhaustion
--- explain
Attacking tells you neither *what* is targeted nor *how fast*. The Allies in the Second World War were advancing on the Axis capitals, and
Echevarria calls their strategy **attrition** [1, Ch. 3]. Rome besieging Carthaginian cities in Sicily was attacking, and sieges are a
form of **exhaustion** [1, Ch. 3]. Napoleon's offensive at Ulm was **annihilation** [1, Ch. 2].
:::

The reverse is true too: a defender can fight an annihilation battle. In 255 BCE, Carthage was the *defender* in Africa when
Xanthippus, a Spartan mercenary, used elephants and cavalry to destroy the Roman invasion army: Polybius says only about 2,000
Romans escaped [2, 1.32–34]. One big battle that eliminated the enemy force: annihilation, by the side defending its home.

### Levels: a battle inside a campaign inside a war
Your instinct wasn't wrong. It was aimed at the wrong level. Echevarria nests the types [1, Ch. 1]:

> "we might design a battle of encirclement and annihilation as part of a campaign of dislocation, which, in turn, might contribute
> to an overall strategy of attrition or exhaustion."

::: diagram kind=graph dir=right caption="Levels of war (Echevarria Ch. 1): each level can use a different type."
- b: Battle: encirclement and annihilation
- c: Campaign: dislocation
- w: War: attrition or exhaustion
- b > c: part of
- c > w: contributes to
:::

So each claim has to say which level it's about. Cannae is an annihilation **battle**; the Second Punic War around it became a
long struggle for allies, a slow **war** [1, Ch. 2]. A quick strategy that doesn't break the enemy's will turns into a slow one.

::: categorize punic-levels
Your war, phase by phase (Polybius, Book I [2]). Which type is each, by its main mechanism?
- 256 BCE: Regulus invades Africa, counting on taking Carthage "in a very short time" > Annihilation
- 241 BCE: the whole 24-year war, which Rome won by outlasting Carthage > Attrition
- Rome lays a long siege to Lilybaeum, Carthage's stronghold in Sicily > Exhaustion
- 255 BCE: Xanthippus destroys Regulus's army in one battle > Annihilation
--- explain
Regulus's invasion was a bet on a quick knockout (and it failed); Xanthippus's battle was an annihilation by the defender; a siege
is a form of exhaustion [1, Ch. 3]; and the war as a whole was won by grinding down Carthage's capacity to fight, in the end its
ability to supply its forces by sea [2, 1.62]. You can argue the whole-war line as exhaustion, since Carthage chose to make peace,
but your analysis feedback showed its will held while its capacity broke.
:::

::: find-error punic-argument
Find the step where this argument goes wrong.
- Rome fought aggressively for 24 years, invading Sicily, Corsica and Sardinia.
- [x] An aggressive war of conquest is a strategy of annihilation.
- So Rome's way in the First Punic War was annihilation.
- So Rome expected to win in one or two battles.
--- explain
Step 2 confuses *attacking* with a *type*. Annihilation is defined by speed (one or two battles) and target (material strength),
not by who attacks [1, Ch. 2]. Step 4 is the giveaway: Rome clearly didn't expect that; the war lasted 24 years.
:::
