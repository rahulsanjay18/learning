Learning · Military strategy · Lesson 1 · Reference: types of strategy

Capacity or will?

Sort any military strategy into one of four basic types by asking two questions. About 40 minutes: 20 reading, 20 practice.

Why this comes first

You want to analyze real campaigns and say why they worked or failed. That needs a vocabulary for what a side was trying to do before you can judge whether it did it. Echevarria opens his book with an exchange from 1975. US Colonel Harry Summers said to a North Vietnamese colonel: “You know you never defeated us on the battlefield.” Colonel Tu replied: “That may be so, but it is also irrelevant.”1 Both men were right, and today's lesson explains how: the two sides were running different kinds of strategy.

Today's win: given a description of a campaign, you can name its strategy type (annihilation, dislocation, attrition, or exhaustion) and say which two questions decided it.

2 · The idea in brief

Echevarria defines military strategy as the practice of reducing an adversary's physical capacity and willingness to fight, and continuing to do so until one's aim is achieved1. Two targets are in that sentence:

  • Capacity: the enemy's physical, material ability to fight (troops, ships, factories).
  • Will: the enemy's willingness to keep fighting (morale, public support, a government's resolve).

He then pairs the classic strategies by how fast they try to win. Annihilation and dislocation seek a swift victory; attrition and exhaustion accept that the process of defeating an opponent may take considerable time1. Cross the two and you get a 2×2:

Aims at capacityAims at will
Quick
(one battle or campaign)		 AnnihilationDestroy the enemy's forces in one or two big battles, often by encirclement. DislocationAn unexpected move or surprise that throws the enemy off balance psychologically.
Slow
(prolonged)		 AttritionDestroy forces faster than the enemy can replace them, while keeping your own losses bearable. ExhaustionWear down the enemy's willingness: make the war seem endless, pointless, or unwinnable.

The cell definitions paraphrase Echevarria, Chapters 1–31. The 2×2 layout is my own arrangement of his pairs; he presents them as two pairs of “polar opposites”.

Two cautions from Echevarria, so you don't over-trust the grid:

  1. The pairs blur in practice. He says annihilation and dislocation often work hand-in-glove, and attrition and exhaustion are hard to tell apart too1. Classify by the main mechanism the strategist was counting on.
  2. Quick strategies often turn into slow ones. Perhaps the greatest risk … lies in assuming a major military victory will prove decisive. A shocked enemy may sue for peace, or may dig in, and then the war becomes attrition or exhaustion1.

Where this sits: ends, ways, means

The most common working model in Western defense education is Arthur Lykke's (1989): strategy = ends (objectives) + ways (courses of action) + means (resources), often with risk added12. Echevarria notes that ways are essentially types of military strategy1, so the 2×2 is your first vocabulary for the ways slot. His example: NATO in central Europe. End: deter a Warsaw Pact attack. Way: strong defensive postures. Means: nuclear, conventional, and special forces1.

Games and AI angle (my analogy, not Echevarria's). A chess engine scores material and mating chances, which is pure capacity. It never models whether the opponent will resign. Real wars often end on the will axis first, which is why historical wargames usually add something like a morale or national-will track, or victory points, on top of unit strength. When you look at a strategy game, ask how its designers represented the will column, if at all.

3 · Practice: classify the campaign

Every case below is one Echevarria discusses in Chapters 2–31. Ask the two questions in order: capacity or will? then quick or slow?

Ulm, 1805. Napoleon fixes the Austrian General Mack's attention with a feint, swings his main army round Mack's flank and rear, cuts his communications to Vienna, and takes the surrender of 27,000 men in a single campaign.

Capacity, quick. Echevarria calls Ulm “a battle of annihilation on a grand scale”, and a rare case where annihilation actually won the war.

France, 1940. Germany has no better technology than France and Britain. It attacks through the Ardennes, thought impassable for armor, reaches the Channel in ten days, and the French premier tells the senate “our classic conception of war has come up against a new conception.”

Will, quick. Echevarria calls it “a definitive example of a strategy of dislocation”. The point was the psychological shock of the unexpected, not out-destroying the Allies.

The Pacific, 1943. Roosevelt tells Congress the primary task is “a day-by-day and week-by-week and month-by-month destruction of more Japanese war materials than Japanese industry could replace.”

Capacity, slow. That is almost word for word Echevarria's definition of attrition: destroying forces faster than they can be replaced.

Indochina, 1946. Ho Chi Minh warns a French general that although ten Vietnamese will die for every Frenchman killed, the French will tire of the struggle first.

Will, slow. He openly accepts losing the capacity race and bets on French will running out first. Echevarria uses it to introduce exhaustion.

1914–1918. The Royal Navy's “Hunger Blockade” cuts German imports by more than half. Most estimates put German deaths from starvation above 750,000.

Exhaustion. Echevarria lists blockades meant to starve a population as “typical of a strategy of exhaustion”, aimed at bringing the population “to its knees”. It also hurt war production, the capacity side, which is the hand-in-glove blur again.

Manila Bay, 1898. Seven modern US warships destroy a Spanish flotilla of about forty older vessels in a day, making repeated runs while dodging most of the return fire.

Capacity, quick: annihilation, driven by a technology gap. Note the sting in the tail. Spain still did not sue for peace until land forces took Manila and Santiago. Destroying the fleet wasn't enough on its own.

Italy, after 217 BCE. Fabius Maximus, nicknamed “the Delayer”, avoids pitched battle with Hannibal and goes after his supplies, “hitting the enemy army in the stomach.”

Exhaustion. Echevarria classes Fabius, and Washington, the “American Fabius”, as exhaustion: wear the enemy down over time instead of risking a decisive battle. Hitting supplies also touches capacity, but the strategy relied on time and avoiding battle.

Judgment: when the grid isn't the whole story

Cannae, 216 BCE, is the textbook annihilation: about 50,000 Romans killed in a double envelopment. Yet Rome won the war fourteen years later. Why didn't Cannae decide it?

Hannibal destroyed capacity but Rome's willingness didn't break. Rome refused to surrender, and the war turned into a long struggle for allies. That is a quick strategy becoming a slow one, which Echevarria calls “the classic pitfall of winning battles only to lose wars.”

Back to Summers and Tu. Which pairing best explains “never defeated on the battlefield … but irrelevant”?

The US aimed at capacity and won most battles. North Vietnam aimed at American will, and Echevarria cites Gallup: in August 1965, 61% of Americans said the war was not a mistake; by October 1967 that was 44%, and by the end of 1967 only 39% approved of how the war was being handled. Winning the capacity contest didn't matter once the will contest was lost.

4 · Retrieve it

Close your eyes for ten seconds, then write the grid without scrolling up. Retrieving it now is what makes it stick next week.

Write the 2×2: the two axes, and which strategy goes in each cell. Then add one historical example per cell.

Axes: capacity vs. will (what it targets) and quick vs. slow (how long it expects to take). Quick + capacity = annihilation (Ulm, Manila Bay). Quick + will = dislocation (France 1940). Slow + capacity = attrition (Allies in WWII). Slow + will = exhaustion (Ho Chi Minh, Fabius, the Hunger Blockade).

5 · Take it outside

Pick any war or campaign you're curious about, ancient or current. For each side, write three lines:

  1. End: what did they want politically?
  2. Way: which cell were they mainly counting on? Did it shift over the war?
  3. Means: what did they actually have to do it with?
